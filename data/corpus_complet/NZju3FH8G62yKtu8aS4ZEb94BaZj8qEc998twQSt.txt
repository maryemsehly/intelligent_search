GENERAL INTRODUCTION
In today’s digital landscape, the exponential growth of information has created
significant challenges in file organization and retrieval. As individuals and orga-
nizations accumulate vast collections of documents, images, and media on multi-
ple platforms, traditional manual classification methods have become increasingly
inadequate. The conventional approach of creating folder hierarchies, manually
naming files, and attempting to maintain consistent organizational systems often
breaks down under the sheer volume and variety of digital content. This results
in scattered information, duplicated files, inconsistent naming conventions, and
ultimately reduced productivity as users spend valuable time searching for specific
content.
The consequences of poor file organization extend beyond mere inconvenience.
In professional environments, misplaced documents can lead to missed deadlines,
compliance issues, and impaired decision-making. For individuals, disorganized
personal files create frustration and inefficiency in daily tasks.
As our digital
footprints continue to expand across devices and platforms, the limitations of
manual classification become increasingly apparent, highlighting the need for more
sophisticated approaches to information management.
Artificial intelligence offers a transformative solution to these challenges
through its ability to understand content rather than rely on explicit human catego-
rization. By leveraging advanced algorithms and learning capabilities, AI-powered
classification systems can analyze the actual content of files—whether textual doc-
uments or visual images—to determine their nature, relevance, and appropriate
categorization. This content-based approach represents a fundamental shift from
systems that require users to adapt to rigid organizational structures toward in-
telligent solutions that adapt to users’ needs and content patterns.
The foundation of these intelligent systems lies in technologies such as machine

CHAPTER 1
GENERAL FRAMEWORK OF THE PROJECT
1.1
Introduction
The proliferation of digital information in the personal and professional domains
presents significant challenges to effective organization and retrieval. Individuals
and organizations alike are grappling with ever-increasing volumes of files in various
formats, including text documents, images, presentations, and scanned materials.
Traditional methods of manual file sorting, naming conventions, and folder hierar-
chies often prove inadequate, leading to inefficiency, inconsistency, and difficulty
in locating specific information when needed. This project addresses these chal-
lenges by proposing an intelligent system, named NosyBot, designed to automate
the classification of digital files based on their semantic content rather than relying
solely on metadata or filenames. The system aims to handle both text and image
files, operate effectively in multilingual environments (English, French), function
entirely offline to ensure data privacy, and adapt over time with minimal human
supervision.
1.2
Problem Statement
Manual organization of digital files into meaningful categories is inherently time
consuming and susceptible to human error and inconsistency. As the volume of
digital information expands exponentially, the reliance on manual methods be-
comes increasingly impractical and inefficient. Furthermore, existing automated
solutions often struggle to handle the heterogeneity of modern file types, partic-
ularly the mix of textual and visual data, failing to realize the full potential of

of understanding the underlying meaning of files regardless of their format.
1.3
Project Objective
The primary objective of this project is to develop and evaluate a practical, effi-
cient, and scalable AI-powered system, NosyBot, capable of:
• Automatically classifying documents and images based on their semantic
content, moving beyond superficial features such as filenames or extensions.
• Supporting a diverse range of file types, including native text documents,
scanned documents (via Optical Character Recognition (OCR)), and various
image formats.
• Operating effectively in multilingual contexts, specifically handling content
in Arabic, French, and English.
• Leveraging principles from few-shot and zero-shot learning to minimize de-
pendency on large, manually labeled datasets for category definition.
• Having the capability to adapt and potentially improve its classification ac-
curacy through mechanisms that incorporate user feedback(though full con-
tinuous learning is beyond the current scope).
• Performing entirely offline, ensuring data sovereignty and suitability for en-
vironments with strict data privacy requirements or limited internet connec-
tivity.
1.4
Selection of Methodology
To structure the development process for this project, the CRISP-DM methodol-
ogy(Cross Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) was selected. CRISP-DM
provides a structured, yet flexible and iterative framework well suited for data-
centric projects such as this one [1]. Its iterative nature allows for refinement at
each stage, ensuring a systematic approach to achieving the project objectives.
The standard phases of the CRISP-DM model, illustrated in Figure 1.1, were
adapted as follows for the NosyBot project:

• Business Understanding: This initial phase focused on clearly defining
the project’s goals and translating the need for better file organization into

• Data Preparation: This phase concentrated on transforming the raw data
into a suitable format for classification models. Activities included perform-
ing OCR on scanned documents, potentially cleaning extracted text, gener-
ating descriptive captions or feature embeddings for images, and preparing
reference examples for each target category.
• Modeling: Here, the core classification logic was developed. This involved
selecting the appropriate techniques and models to compare new files with
the reference examples. Different strategies and combinations were explored
to achieve robust classification.

1.5
System Naming: NosyBot
The name "NosyBot" was chosen to reflect the core function and character of the
system. Analogously to how a curious individual might investigate details, Nosy-
Bot is designed to "scrutinize" file content with algorithmic diligence to understand
its meaning. Examines files, analyzes their content semantically, and determines

Zero-shot Learning
Zero-shot Learning [48] enables models to perform tasks without any task-specific
examples, relying entirely on their pre-trained knowledge to generate relevant re-
sponses. This approach proves particularly valuable when rapid classification or
captioning results are needed without available training data. For instance, im-
age captioning models can generate descriptive text for previously unseen images
based solely on their pre-training. Zero-shot learning demonstrates the remarkable
transfer capabilities of modern AI systems, allowing them to generalize knowledge
to novel, unseen tasks.
2.5.2
One-shot Learning
One-shot Learning [49] enables models to generalize from just a single example,
making it particularly valuable when reference material is scarce. By extracting
key patterns from one exemplar, the model can effectively categorize new inputs.
This approach strikes a balance between implementation simplicity and accuracy,
especially for categories with consistent characteristics. Mirroring human ability
to learn from minimal examples, one-shot learning offers an efficient paradigm for
rapid deployment of classification systems like NosyBot.
2.5.3
Few-shots Learning
Few-shot learning extends this by providing several examples (typically 2 to 5)
within the prompt. This often leads to better performance than zero-shot or one-
shot learning by giving the model more context and examples of the desired task
execution [3]. NosyBot’s approach of using reference files for classification aligns
conceptually with few-shot principles, as it relies on a small number of examples
(references) to define categories.

Deep Learning
Deep Learning (DL) is a subset of ML based on artificial neural networks with mul-
tiple layers (deep architectures) between the input and output layers. These layers
allow the model to learn hierarchical representations of the data, which makes DL
particularly effective for complex tasks involving large amounts of unstructured
data such as text and images [6].

2.6.1
Convolutional Neural Networks
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are a class of deep neural networks that
is particularly well suited to processing grid-like data, such as images. They use
convolutional layers to automatically and adaptively learn spatial hierarchies of
features, from edges and textures in the early layers to more complex objects in
deeper layers. CNNs have been foundamental in computer vision tasks [5].
2.6.2
Transformers
Transformers are a more recent deep learning architecture that has revolutionized
NLP and is increasingly applied to other domains such as computer vision [14].
They rely heavily on the mechanism of "attention," particularly "self-attention,"
which allows the model to weigh the importance of different parts of the input
data (e.g. words in a sentence) when processing information. Key components
include:
• Self-Attention: Enables the model to look at other words in the input
sentence as it processes a specific word, capturing contextual relationships.
• Multi-Head Attention: Runs the attention mechanism multiple times in
parallel, allowing the model to jointly attend to information from different
representation subspaces.
• Positional Encoding: Injects information about the position of elements in
the sequence, as the core architecture does not inherently process sequential
order.
• Encoder-Decoder Structure: Many transformers consist of an encoder
(to process the input sequence) and a decoder (to generate the output se-
quence), though some models use only the encoder (like BERT) or only the
decoder (like GPT).

Computer Vision deals with enabling machines to "see" and interpret visual infor-

2.9
Fundamentals of File Classification
File classification is the process of categorizing files into groups based on their
content or attributes. There are three primary methods for classifying files:
2.9.1
Rule-based Classification Approach
This method uses manually defined rules, often based on metadata like file ex-
tension (e.g., .txt -> Text, .jpg -> Image), filename patterns or simple keyword
presence. It is simple and fast, but brittle, inflexible, and struggles with content
understanding.
2.9.2
Content-based Classification Approach
This approach classifies files according to their actual content, analyzing the text
in documents or the visual elements in images. It is more robust and meaningful,
but requires more sophisticated techniques.
2.9.3
Hybrid Approach
This combines rule-based and content-based methods. For instance, rules might
quickly identify file types (image vs. text document), directing the file to a spe-
cialized content-based analysis pipeline (CV or NLP). NosyBot employs a hybrid
strategy: it uses rules for initial type detection and then applies advanced content-
based (semantic) analysis using appropriate ML/DL models.

pipeline, particularly for the training approach. This method uses the sentence-
transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model to:
1. Generate embeddings for image captions produced by BLIP.
2. Generate embeddings for reference examples and category descriptions.
3. Calculate cosine similarity between these embeddings.
4. Create a semantic understanding of image content in relation to categories.
The all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model was specifically selected for this task due to its
excellent balance of performance and efficiency. As a distilled model with 384-
dimensional embeddings, it provides high-quality semantic representations while
maintaining reasonable computational requirements for offline processing. This

Abstract
Abstract
Managing and organizing mixed file types like images and
documents can be frustrating and time-consuming, especially
when it’s done manually.
This project introduces NosyBot,
an AI-based agent built to automatically classify both image
and text files by understanding their actual content — not
just their name or format.
It uses simple rule-based logic to
detect the file type, then applies the right pipeline: captioning
for visual files, or text extraction and analysis for documents.
From there, it compares each file to reference examples using
semantic similarity.
There’s also a fallback model for cases
where things aren’t clear.
While the system is still being
improved, early testing shows that NosyBot works reliably in
real-time and handles files with minimal user input. The goal is
to make intelligent file sorting faster, easier, and a lot less manual.
Keywords:
file classification, AI agent, semantic similarity,
rule-based logic, text and image files, content-aware sorting,
automatic organization, fallback model, real-time processing
