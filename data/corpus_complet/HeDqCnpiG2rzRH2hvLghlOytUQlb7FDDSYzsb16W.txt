General Introduction
In the legal profession, efficient organization and access to information
are critical to success. Law firms handle a vast array of documents,
correspondences, evidence, and legal filings for multiple cases at once. To
manage this complex workload, many firms rely on case folder
management tools. These tools serve as centralized systems that store,
organize, and track case-related information, enabling legal professionals to
work more effectively and with greater accuracy.
The primary purpose of a case folder management tool is to streamline
the documentation process by digitizing and centralizing all case materials.
Instead of relying on physical folders or scattered digital files, these systems
offer a structured platform where case files can be categorized, labeled,
and retrieved quickly. This organization reduces the risk of losing vital
documents, improves collaboration among team members, and ensures
that all case information is readily accessible, even remotely.
A typical case folder includes several types of information. At its core, it
contains client details such as contact information, identification records,
and signed agreements. It also stores all case documentation, including
pleadings, motions, discovery materials, evidence files, court transcripts,
and legal research. Correspondence—whether with clients, opposing
counsel, or the court—is also archived for reference. Many tools allow for
the inclusion of timelines, task lists, and notes to support workflow
management and case strategy planning.
Ultimately, case folder management tools provide law firms with a
reliable, efficient way to handle increasing caseloads and complex litigation
requirements.

1.2 General Context
1.2.1 Problem
Despite their many benefits, case folder management tools in law firms
face several challenges. One major issue is the lack of standardization
across platforms, which can lead to compatibility problems when sharing
files between systems or collaborating with external parties. Additionally,
some tools can be complex to use, requiring significant training for staff and
leading to resistance or inconsistent usage. Security remains a pressing
concern, especially with the risk of cyberattacks targeting sensitive client
data. Smaller firms may also struggle with the high cost of implementing
and maintaining these tools, limiting their access to the most advanced
features. These issues highlight the need for more user-friendly, secure, and
affordable solutions tailored to the unique demands of legal work.

1.4.1 Project Management Approaches: Agile vs. Traditional
When it comes to project management, two approaches stand out: the
Agile approach and the traditional approach.
The Agile approach emphasizes adaptability, collaboration, and iteration.
Teams work in short cycles, called "sprints," where they focus on specific
objectives and achieve frequent deliveries of functional products.
In contrast, the traditional approach follows a linear and sequential
approach, with detailed planning from the beginning of the project. It
involves less iteration and flexibility compared to the Agile approach.
Each approach has its advantages and is suited to specific project contexts.
In the following section, we will choose the method that best suits our
project, taking into account its specific characteristics and our needs.
1.4.2 Choosing a Method
To improve our project management, it is essential to adopt an
appropriate method.

on comparing the Waterfall, V-cycle, and Scrum methods, which are widely
used in business.
The Waterfall method, also known as the waterfall model, consists of
following a series of pre-established steps sequentially, where each step

1.4.4.1 Definition of Scrum
Scrum is a flexible and collaborative approach to project management
that emphasizes iterative and incremental development. It focuses on
delivering high-value products through short, time-bound iterations called
sprints, during which multidisciplinary teams work together to deliver a
potentially shippable product increment at the end of each sprint.
1.4.4.2 Key Principles of Scrum
• Transparency: All aspects of the process must be visible and
understood by all stakeholders.
• Inspection: Progress toward objectives must be regularly inspected to
identify unwanted variations.
• Adaptation: If an inspection reveals a deviation from the objective,
adjustments must be made to minimize the deviation.
1.4.4.3 Roles in Scrum
• Product Owner: The Product Owner is responsible for maximizing the
value of the product and the development team's work. They define and
prioritize Product Backlog items based on the value they bring to the
product.
• Scrum Master: The Scrum Master is responsible for ensuring the
Scrum Team understands and adheres to the values, practices, and rules of
Scrum. They help the team resolve problems and continually improve their
effectiveness.
• Development Team: The Development Team is responsible for
regularly delivering usable product features. They are self-organized and

• Generate client: This function allows the secretary to create and

sessions. It helps secretaries view upcoming sessions by day, week, or
month and serves as a planning tool for lawyers. The calendar is
automatically updated when new sessions are created or modified.

archive the entire case folder. Archiving moves the folder out of the active
