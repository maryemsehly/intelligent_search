Abstract
Organizations contend with rapidly evolving cyber threats that outpace signature-based security
solutions. This project delivers and critically evaluates a practical MLDevSecOps pipeline that
integrates open-source Suricata IDS/IPS, the ELK Stack, and unsupervised anomaly detection
methods specifically Z-Score statistical thresholding and Isolation Forest for near real-time
intrusion detection and automated response. The platform is deployed across five virtual machines
dedicated to network monitoring (Suricata), log aggregation (ELK), machine learning analysis,
CI/CD orchestration (Jenkins), and simulated attack testing (OWASP Juice Shop). Network
events are continuously collected and streamed[2], enriched via Elasticsearch, and analyzed
using Z-Score for outlier detection in numeric fields and Isolation Forest for multivariate anomaly
identification. Detected anomalies and blocking actions are automatically logged, with confirmed
threats triggering OS-level IP address blocking using iptables. Model retraining is currently
performed manually based on data thresholds, and all evaluation is carried out in a realistic
but controlled lab setting. Experimental validation demonstrates effective detection and timely
response to simulated attacks using these two methods alone. This work provides a robust,
extensible foundation for integrating IDS, SIEM, streaming, and lightweight unsupervised
machine learning within an MLDevSecOps workflow, with clear opportunities for automating
retraining and expanding analytical capabilities in production environments.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1
General Context
Over the past decade, the digital transformation of organizations has embedded networked
systems into nearly every aspect of business activity, from industrial operations to cloud-based
services and remote collaboration tools. This growing reliance on interconnected infrastructure
has greatly expanded the potential impact of cybersecurity incidents, making modern organi-
zations vulnerable to threats that can disrupt operations, expose sensitive data, and undermine
public trust.
Adversaries now employ a diverse mix of attack techniques including phishing, lateral
movement, privilege escalation, and data exfiltration to execute targeted, multi-stage campaigns.
The proliferation of cloud-native deployments, virtualization, containerization, and microservices
have led to highly dynamic IT environments, with frequent configuration changes and ephemeral
workloads complicating monitoring and attack detection.
Traditional, perimeter-based network defenses and rule-driven detection systems struggle
to keep pace with this complexity. Signature-based approaches are increasingly inadequate
against zero-day vulnerabilities, polymorphic malware, and coordinated attacks engineered to
evade static rules[20]. Simultaneously, the massive volume and diversity of security telemetry
produced in modern environments quickly overwhelm manual inspection and reactive response
workflows.
To address these challenges, there is a shift toward integrating lightweight, unsupervised
anomaly detection methods into security operations. Rather than relying solely on static signa-
tures and human-driven triage, organizations are exploring automated pipelines that continuously
collect, correlate, and enrich telemetry data, applying statistical and unsupervised learning
techniques to surface deviations from normal behavior in near real time. This work leverages
two such methods—Z-Score for univariate outlier detection and Isolation Forest for multivariate
anomaly identification within an MLDevSecOps framework. By embedding these techniques
into DevSecOps practices, the pipeline enables security controls that are both automated and
adaptive, capable of identifying and responding to suspicious activity across complex, distributed
environments without dependence on labeled threat data or deep learning models.
1.2
Motivation
Recent global analyses reveal a sustained increase in the frequency, sophistication, and financial
impact of cyber incidents. The average cost of a data breach hovers around $4.44 million

===== PAGE 12 =====

worldwide in 2025, reaching as high as $10.22 million in the United States, encompassing
remediation expenses, regulatory fines, legal liabilities, and reputational damage[8, 21]. Attackers
often gain prolonged undetected access spanning weeks or months enabling them to escalate
privileges and exfiltrate sensitive information covertly.
Simultaneously, security operations centers (SOCs) are inundated by voluminous logs, alerts,
and network events generated by diverse sources such as firewalls, intrusion detection systems,
and endpoint agents. High false-positive rates overwhelm analysts, leading to missed critical
threats or delayed responses. Traditional manual triage and rule-based correlation techniques are
no longer sufficient to manage the scale and complexity of modern infrastructures.
These overarching challenges underscore the necessity for cyber defense architectures that
integrate high-performance network monitoring, centralized log aggregation, scalable data
processing, and lightweight, unsupervised anomaly detection. This project leverages two such
techniques—Z-Score for statistical outlier detection in individual metrics and Isolation Forest
for identifying anomalous patterns across multivariate telemetry—to surface suspicious activity
without reliance on labeled data or predefined attack signatures. By embedding these methods
into security pipelines, organizations can reduce detection latency, filter noise from high-volume
alerts, and support faster analyst response.
Through the MLDevSecOps paradigm, this work explores how even simple, interpretable
anomaly detection models can be systematically integrated with infrastructure automation to
enable reproducible, responsive threat identification. The project is motivated by the imperative
to experimentally validate such integrated frameworks in controlled, realistic network environ-
ments—laying groundwork for future enhancements while demonstrating near-term utility using
only Z-Score and Isolation Forest.
1.3
Problem Statement
Despite the availability of robust intrusion detection systems, SIEM platforms, and automation
tools, many modern security monitoring solutions still face significant challenges that undermine
their effectiveness in today’s complex IT environments:
• Signature dependency: Conventional IDS/IPS engines are highly reliant on static attack
signatures, rendering them insufficient for detecting novel exploits, polymorphic malware,
and sophisticated, long-duration attacks engineered to bypass rule-based defenses.
• Fragmented log management: Security telemetry is typically scattered across disparate
systems and formats, hindering correlation, holistic visibility, and effective threat analysis.
• Overwhelming telemetry volume: The massive volume and velocity of network and
application logs produced in distributed, cloud-centric, and high-throughput environments
make manual inspection infeasible and strain the capabilities of conventional correlation
engines[17].
• Scalability and performance limitations: Many traditional monitoring architectures
were designed for static, on-premises deployments and struggle to process and analyze
data in real time as infrastructure becomes more dynamic and geographically distributed.
• Manual and delayed response: The transition from detection to mitigation frequently
depends on human analysts, creating critical delays during which attackers can escalate
privileges, move laterally, and cause further harm to the organization.

Collectively, these limitations reveal the necessity for integrated solutions that seamlessly

1.4
Project Objectives
The primary aim of this project is to design, build, and evaluate an MLDevSecOps pipeline that
demonstrates the viability of intelligent, automated network threat detection and response using
open-source technologies. The specific objectives are:
• Establish a real-time, integrated observability stack:
– Deploy Suricata for deep network traffic inspection and alert generation.
– Utilize the ELK Stack for centralized collection, parsing, indexing, and visualization
of security events.
– Implement statistical and machine learning models (Z-Score, Isolation Forest) to
highlight and score anomalous patterns in network telemetry.
• Enable automated detection-to-response workflows:
– Automate end-to-end detection, including log preprocessing, feature extraction,
anomaly scoring, and alerting.
– Execute response actions, such as OS-level IP blocking via iptables, immediately
upon detecting high-confidence anomalies, minimizing manual analyst intervention.
• Support auditable operations and continuous improvement:
– Log all detected anomalies and mitigation actions for forensic review and system
improvement.

• Ensure modularity and future scalability:
– Lay the groundwork for scalable data streaming and analytics (e.g., Kafka backbone,
multiple analytical consumers).
– Prepare the architecture for future integration of additional analytical methods, while
currently relying exclusively on Z-Score and Isolation Forest.
• Expose operational and analytical insights:
– Provide the means (present or future placeholders) for dashboard-based visibility
into system health, model performance, detection outcomes, and resource utilization.
This set of objectives anchors the project’s scope to practical, hands-on implementation while
establishing a foundation for future extensibility and research.

1.5
Adopted Methodology
To ensure a rigorous, reproducible, and practically relevant outcome, the project adopts a
structured engineering and evaluation process combining conceptual analysis with hands-on
implementation. The major phases of this methodology are:
1. Literature review and technology assessment: Conduct a thorough survey of current
IDS/IPS platforms, SIEM solutions, streaming frameworks, and relevant machine learning
approaches for security. Particular attention is paid to integration strategies and limitations
of existing tools.
2. Requirements specification: Define formal functional and non-functional requirements
for the proposed system, including attack types to be covered, detection and response
workflows, scalability targets, detection latency, and reliability needs.
3. Architectural design: Develop a layered architecture integrating Suricata, ELK Stack,
machine learning modules, and automation tools. Explicitly specify data flows, key
integration points, component responsibilities, and the overall security model.
4. Incremental implementation and integration: Build and configure the platform step by
step, beginning with basic log collection and visualization. Successive phases introduce
streaming infrastructure, anomaly detection services, active response mechanisms, and
monitoring capabilities. All components are deployed on dedicated virtual machines for
isolation and repeatability.
5. Experimental validation: Rigorously test the system by generating realistic background
traffic and conducting simulated attacks (e.g., via the OWASP Juice Shop). Measure not
only detection quality and false positive rates, but also performance characteristics such as
processing throughput and detection/response latency.
6. Analysis and documentation: Critically assess experimental results, identify operational
limitations, and document both the architectural design and lessons learned. Propose
specific future enhancements for scalability, automation, and analytic depth.
This methodology ensures that the proposed solution is both grounded in current research
and best practices, and thoroughly vetted through empirical experimentation in conditions that
approximate real-world security challenges.
1.6
Scope and Limitations
This project is intentionally scoped to demonstrate the core integration and practical viability
of the MLDevSecOps approach within a controlled laboratory environment. The following
boundaries and limitations are acknowledged:
• Implemented ML Models: Only statistical (Z-Score) and unsupervised (Isolation Forest)
algorithms are fully operational in the current pipeline. Advanced approaches such as au-
toencoders, LSTM networks, or graph-based models are discussed as future enhancements
and are not part of the present implementation.

• Infrastructure and Deployment: All system components are deployed on dedicated
virtual machines within a single hypervisor for repeatability and ease of management.
The platform does not address enterprise-scale production issues such as multi-cloud
deployment, geographic distribution, or multi-tenant isolation.
• Retraining and Automation: Model retraining is currently initiated manually once a
predefined log volume is reached (e.g., 500 records). Continuous, automated retraining
pipelines and seamless integration with CI/CD for ML updates remain a future goal[?, 14].
• Interface and Usability: Analyst dashboards and manual labeling interfaces are designed
in concept but not fully implemented. Log file analysis is used for most evaluations and
forensic review.
• Data Scope: Evaluation is performed using a mix of synthetic, benign, and controlled
attack traffic (including OWASP Juice Shop scenarios)[18]. Host-based telemetry and
application logs are not yet integrated—focus remains on network-level events.
• Model Robustness and Adversarial Resilience: While the system reports detection
accuracy and false positive rates, it has not been evaluated against adversarial tactics such
as model evasion or poisoning, nor against large-scale drift in traffic behaviors[6].
• Regulatory, Privacy, and Compliance: Security best practices are adopted at a technical
level, but no attempt is made to implement or audit for specific regulatory or privacy
compliance frameworks in this prototype.
These limitations are necessary in order to maintain focus and feasibility, allowing the project
to robustly demonstrate practical integration and functionality while also providing guidance for
future research and scaling toward production use cases.

Chapter 2
State of the Art
2.1
Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDS/IPS)
Network intrusion detection and prevention systems are core building blocks of modern cyberse-
curity architectures. These systems continuously inspect network traffic to identify malicious
activities, either notifying analysts in IDS mode or actively blocking suspicious flows in IPS
mode.
Among open-source solutions, Snort and Suricata are two of the most widely deployed
engines. Snort was first released in 1998 and has evolved into one of the most established
IDS/IPS tools in both industry and academia. Suricata, introduced by the Open Information
Security Foundation (OISF) around 2009, was designed from the outset to leverage multi-core
hardware and generate rich, structured telemetry suitable for large-scale analysis[12]. Both can
operate at high speeds, but traditional deployments often rely heavily on signature-based rules,
which makes it difficult to detect novel or obfuscated attacks.
2.1.1
Suricata
Suricata is a high-performance, open-source IDS/IPS and network security monitoring engine
developed and maintained by the OISF. It supports native multi-threading and deep packet
inspection, allowing it to fully utilize modern multi-core CPUs and to parse a wide range of
application-layer protocols[11, 12].
A key distinguishing feature of Suricata is its EVE JSON logging format. This structured
output includes detailed information on flows, alerts, anomalies, files, TLS handshakes, HTTP
transactions, and more. As a result, Suricata logs can be ingested directly by SIEM platforms,
big data frameworks, and machine learning pipelines with minimal preprocessing. Suricata is
commonly used in enterprise SOCs, research testbeds, and open-source security distributions
such as Security Onion, where its scalability and log richness are critical.

2.1.2
Snort
Snort, originally developed by Martin Roesch in 1998 and later acquired and maintained by Cisco,
is one of the oldest and most mature IDS/IPS engines. It relies primarily on signature-based
detection with a large, community-driven rule set and has been integrated into many commercial
and open-source security products.
For many years, Snort 2.x used a largely single-threaded processing model, which can be-
come a bottleneck in very high-bandwidth environments. Snort 3.x introduced a more modular,
multi-threaded architecture, but Suricata has generally been perceived as more aligned with
modern, parallel processing requirements[19]. Snort remains popular in traditional network envi-
ronments, educational labs, and smaller organizations where hardware resources and throughput
requirements are more modest.

2.2
SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)
Tools
Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platforms aggregate, normalize, correlate,
and analyze security-relevant events from across an organization. They provide centralized
visibility, support incident investigations, and help meet compliance requirements. Over the last
decade, SIEM tools have evolved from simple log aggregators into complex analytics platforms
with built-in dashboards, alerting, and, in some cases, machine learning capabilities.
This section focuses on three commonly used SIEM solutions: the ELK Stack, Splunk,
and Graylog. These platforms have seen widespread deployment in SOCs, managed security
services, and cloud-native environments.
The ELK Stack
The ELK Stack (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana) emerged in the early 2010s as a popular open-
source stack for log management and analytics. With the addition of Beats (such as Filebeat), it
provides an end-to-end pipeline for log collection, transformation, indexing, and visualization[5].
• Elasticsearch: A distributed search and analytics engine built on Apache Lucene. It
supports horizontal scaling, high write throughput, and near real-time search, making it a
common choice for centralized logging and SIEM-style workloads.
Figure 2.3: Elasticsearch
• Logstash: A server-side data processing pipeline that ingests, parses, enriches, and
transforms log events before indexing. It supports many input, filter, and output plugins
and is often used as the main “glue” for heterogeneous log sources.
Figure 2.4: Logstash

• Kibana: A visualization and exploration layer that allows analysts to build dashboards,
search logs interactively, and create visualizations for security monitoring and reporting.
Figure 2.5: Kibana
• Filebeat: A lightweight log shipper introduced later in the Beats family that collects log
files from servers and forwards them to Logstash or Elasticsearch.
Figure 2.6: Filebeat

SOCs for threat hunting, incident analysis, and monitoring of IDS alerts.
2.2.1
Splunk
Splunk, first released in 2003, is a commercial platform that provides log management, secu-
rity analytics, and observability capabilities. Splunk Enterprise Security builds on the core
platform with pre-built security dashboards, correlation searches, and compliance reporting
content. Splunk is frequently deployed in large enterprises, financial institutions, and government
environments that require strong vendor support, extensive integrations, and mature governance
features.
Its major strengths include a powerful search language (SPL), rich out-of-the-box content, and
high scalability when appropriately licensed and engineered. However, Splunk’s licensing model,
typically based on daily indexed data volume, can make large-scale deployments expensive,
especially when storing high-volume sources such as full IDS logs or packet metadata.

2.3
Machine Learning for Cybersecurity
Machine learning has become a key component in modern intrusion detection and threat analytics.
Unlike purely signature-based approaches, ML methods can learn patterns from data and detect
behaviors that deviate from expected baselines[4], including zero-day attacks and subtle multi-
stage intrusions.
Typical categories of techniques used in cybersecurity include:
• Statistical anomaly detection: Methods such as z-score analysis flag observations that
deviate significantly from historical baselines.
• Unsupervised learning: Algorithms like Isolation Forest and clustering techniques iden-
tify unusual behavior without requiring labeled attack data[10].
• Deep learning: Models such as LSTMs, autoencoders, and CNNs capture temporal and
non-linear patterns in network flows or logs.
• Graph-based AI: Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) analyze relationships between hosts
and events, making them suitable for detecting lateral movement and coordinated attacks.
While these approaches (e.g., deep learning, graph-based AI) are actively researched, this
project implements only statistical and unsupervised methods: Z-Score and Isolation Forest,
due to their interpretability, low computational overhead, and suitability for real-time anomaly
detection in resource-constrained lab environments[15].
2.3.1
Classical Machine Learning Models
Classical ML methods, such as decision trees, Random Forests, Support Vector Machines
(SVMs), and Isolation Forests, are widely used for intrusion detection on structured features
extracted from flows, packets, or logs. Numerous works have applied these techniques to
benchmark datasets like KDD’99, NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, and CIC-IDS to demonstrate
improvements over traditional rule-based detection.
These models are generally efficient to train and interpret and can achieve good performance
when features are carefully engineered. However, they may struggle to model complex temporal
dependencies or higher-order relationships across hosts and sessions.
2.3.2
Deep Learning Models
Deep learning approaches have been increasingly explored since the mid-2010s for intrusion
detection and malware analysis. LSTMs and other recurrent architectures have been used to
model sequences of events or packets over time, while autoencoders and variational autoencoders
have been applied to unsupervised anomaly detection by reconstructing “normal” behavior and
flagging deviations.
Several studies report detection rates above 95% on specific datasets when using deep
learning models, although these results often depend on curated data and may not fully cap-
ture the challenges of noisy, real-world traffic. Deep learning models typically require more
computational resources and larger, well-prepared datasets than classical methods.

2.3.3
Graph-based AI
Graph-based approaches treat network entities (e.g., hosts, users, processes) as nodes and their
interactions as edges. Graph Neural Networks and related methods can then learn representations
that capture both local and global structural information[4, 1]. This makes them promising for
detecting lateral movement, privilege escalation paths, and coordinated campaigns.
Research prototypes using GNNs for intrusion detection are emerging, but these methods are
not yet as widely adopted in production as classical or deep learning models[17]. They often
require high-quality relationship data and careful modelling of the underlying graph.

2.3.5
DevSecOps and the Emergence of MLDevSecOps
DevOps practices, which became mainstream in the 2010s, emphasize automation, continuous
integration, and continuous delivery (CI/CD) to accelerate software releases. DevSecOps extends
DevOps by integrating security checks—such as static and dynamic code analysis, dependency
scanning, and compliance validation—into build and deployment pipelines, making security a
shared responsibility.
The growing use of machine learning in production systems has led to new operational
challenges: ML models must be trained on evolving data, validated regularly, monitored for drift,
and protected against adversarial manipulation. Traditional DevSecOps practices do not fully
address these ML-specific concerns. This gap has given rise to the concept of MLDevSecOps,
which combines MLOps (for managing the ML lifecycle) with DevSecOps (for embedding
security throughout the pipeline).
MLDevSecOps aims to:
22


===== PAGE 24 =====

• Automate data ingestion, preprocessing, feature extraction, model training, and deploy-
ment.
• Integrate ML-driven anomaly detection and intrusion detection into operational workflows.
• Enforce security controls (e.g., access control, integrity checks) on data, models, and
pipelines.
• Provide continuous monitoring of both system and model performance, with automated
alerts and responses.
Uniqueness of the Proposed Approach
Many existing works focus either on integrating ML
into IDS engines or on building SIEM dashboards that consume IDS alerts. This project differs
by combining:
• A high-performance IDS (Suricata) configured to generate rich JSON telemetry.
• A scalable SIEM stack (ELK) and streaming backbone (Kafka) to transport and store large
volumes of events.
• Two lightweight, unsupervised anomaly detectors—Z-Score for univariate outlier detection
and Isolation Forest for multivariate anomaly identification are integrated directly into the
pipeline, rather than as an offline analysis step.
• An active response mechanism that can modify Suricata rules and block IPs automatically,
closing the loop from detection to mitigation.
• A CI/CD-controlled environment (Jenkins) to manage model deployment, retraining, and
pipeline updates.
This combination results in an MLDevSecOps pipeline tailored specifically for network
intrusion detection and response, with a focus on realistic lab validation using targets such as
OWASP Juice Shop.
2.4
CI/CD and Security in Pipelines
Continuous integration and continuous delivery pipelines are now standard practice for software
development. Tools such as Jenkins, GitLab CI/CD, and GitHub Actions automate building,
testing, and deploying applications, reducing manual errors and supporting faster release cycles.
Incorporating security checks into these pipelines—through DevSecOps practices—helps catch
vulnerabilities earlier and reduce exposure in production.
In the context of MLDevSecOps, CI/CD pipelines not only manage application code but
also handle ML-related assets such as training scripts, model binaries, configuration files, and
infrastructure definitions. This makes the choice of CI/CD tooling important for reproducibility
and security.

Jenkins
Jenkins, initially released in 2005 (as Hudson), is an open-source automation server that has
become a de facto standard for CI/CD in many organizations. Its large plugin ecosystem
allows integration with almost any tool in the software delivery chain, including static analyzers,
container platforms, cloud providers, and test frameworks.
For MLDevSecOps, Jenkins is attractive because it can orchestrate complex workflows
that include data preparation, model training, evaluation, packaging, and deployment to the
monitoring environment[9]. Its flexibility comes at the cost of more manual configuration and
maintenance, especially in larger installations.

GitHub Actions is easy to adopt for projects already hosted on GitHub and is well-suited for

2.5
Related Work
Several research efforts and industrial solutions have attempted to combine IDS, SIEM, and
machine learning for enhanced intrusion detection. For example, some studies use Suricata
or Snort as log sources and train ML classifiers offline on exported features, later comparing
performance with rule-based detection. Others integrate IDS alerts into SIEM platforms like ELK
or Splunk and apply anomaly detection on aggregated events for correlation and prioritization.
Commercial platforms and security products often embed proprietary ML models into their
detection pipelines, but the internal architecture is usually not publicly documented. Many
of these solutions focus on either endpoint detection and response (EDR) or cloud workload
protection rather than on open-source, network-centric pipelines.
Compared to this prior work, the approach in this project is distinct in several ways:
• It uses only open-source components (Suricata, ELK, Kafka, Jenkins, Prometheus, Grafana),
making the architecture transparent and reproducible.
• The ML models are integrated into a streaming pipeline rather than being applied as offline
batch jobs.
• The system includes a closed-loop response mechanism that can automatically update IDS
rules and block traffic based on ML outputs.
25


===== PAGE 27 =====

• The entire pipeline, including ML components, is managed through CI/CD, aligning with
MLDevSecOps principles.
2.6
Conclusion
This chapter reviewed the main technologies and concepts that underpin the proposed MLDe-
vSecOps pipeline: IDS/IPS engines such as Suricata and Snort, SIEM platforms like ELK,
Splunk, and Graylog, machine learning approaches to intrusion detection, and CI/CD tooling for
secure and automated delivery. It also positioned MLDevSecOps as an evolution of DevSecOps
tailored to the operational realities of ML-based systems and highlighted existing efforts to
combine IDS, SIEM, and ML.
The analysis revealed several common limitations in current practice: dependence on static
signatures, limited integration between IDS and big data analytics, challenges in scaling to
high-volume traffic, and a lack of closed-loop automation from detection to response. These
gaps motivate the design of an integrated MLDevSecOps architecture that can process Suricata
logs at scale, apply ML-based anomaly detection in near real time, and automatically react to
detected threats.
The next chapter builds on this foundation by presenting the requirements, design decisions,
and overall architecture of the proposed MLDevSecOps pipeline.

3.1
Requirements Analysis
3.1.1
Functional Requirements
The system is designed to deliver the following core capabilities:
• Network data collection: Ingest network telemetry in near real time using Suricata,
including both regular production-like network traffic and simulated attack flows from the
OWASP Juice Shop VM.
• Log processing and indexing: Process raw logs using Filebeat and Logstash, transform
and enrich events, and store resulting structured data in Elasticsearch for efficient analytics
and search.
• Anomaly detection: Use a combination of statistical (Z-Score) and machine learning
(Isolation Forest) methods to detect behavioral anomalies within the indexed telemetry.
(More advanced models such as autoencoders and LSTMs are noted as planned for future
development, not implemented in the current version.)
• Automated response: Upon confirming high-confidence threats, automatically execute
OS-level IP blocking via iptables to mitigate ongoing attacks. In future extensions, also
support dynamic updates to Suricata detection rules.
• Operational transparency and forensics: Persistently log all detected anomalies and
active responses for auditing, analyst review, and ongoing improvement of ML workflows.
• Retraining and pipeline management: Support manual, data-driven retraining of ML
models when log files reach predefined volume thresholds. Full CI/CD-based retraining
and deployment automation are planned as future enhancements.
• User interfaces and monitoring (planned): Design, but not yet fully implement, dash-
board visualizations (Kibana, Grafana) for system health, detection metrics, and model
performance, as well as interfaces for manual alert review and analyst feedback.

Overview
This chapter presents the experimental validation of the MLDevSecOps platform. The evaluation

5.2
Functional Validation
The system was validated across core operational phases:
• Log collection: Verified that Suricata successfully captures network packets from both

• Log parsing and indexing: Confirmed that Filebeat reliably ships logs to Logstash, which
parses and enriches them before indexing into Elasticsearch without data corruption or
duplication.
• Anomaly detection: Validated that the deployed ensemble of statistical (Z-Score) and
machine learning (Isolation Forest) models correctly identifies suspicious traffic patterns
in near real time.
• Automated response execution: Ensured that the active response subsystem dynamically
blocks malicious source IP addresses via iptables upon high-confidence anomaly detection,
with all actions logged for audit trails and rule persistence verified across VM reboots.
• CI/CD pipeline integration: Jenkins orchestrates a fully operational CI/CD pipeline that
automates infrastructure validation, attack simulation, log ingestion, ML analysis, and
reporting across all VMs. While the pipeline successfully executes end-to-end workflows
and publishes security reports.

Chapter 6
Conclusion and Perspectives
6.1
Project Summary
This work presented an integrated MLDevSecOps framework that unifies high-performance
network intrusion detection, centralized log analytics, scalable event streaming, machine learning-
driven anomaly recognition, and CI/CD-managed automation. The framework combines Suricata
for network inspection, the ELK Stack for log management and visualization, Kafka for dis-
tributed event streaming, multiple complementary ML models for anomaly scoring, and Jenkins
for reproducible pipeline orchestration.
The implementation was deployed across five specialized virtual machines:
• Monitoring VM: Runs Suricata for packet-level traffic analysis and generates structured
EVE JSON security logs. Filebeat ships these logs to downstream consumers.
• ML Processing VM: Hosts feature extraction services and multiple anomaly detection
models including statistical Z-Score analysis for univariate outlier detection and unsuper-
vised Isolation Forest for multivariate anomaly identification.
• ELK Stack VM: Provides centralized log indexing via Elasticsearch, data transformation
via Logstash, and interactive visualization and exploration via Kibana. Serves as the
primary data warehouse for security events and analytical queries.
• OWASP Juice Shop VM: Functions as a deliberately vulnerable web application, gen-
erating realistic and controllable attack traffic for model training, validation, and testing
scenarios.
• Jenkins VM: Serves as the central orchestration engine for the MLDevSecOps pipeline,
automating infrastructure configuration and validation.It executes security attack simu-
lations and coordinates data processing from log collection to machine learning-based
anomaly detection.The system also generates detailed HTML security reports and enforces
pipeline governance through retention policies and notifications.
The resulting pipeline demonstrated robust capability to detect both signature-based attacks
and novel, previously unseen threats in near real-time, execute automated countermeasures
autonomously, and expose comprehensive operational visibility through integrated security and
system monitoring dashboards.

anomaly detection can be achieved without deep learning, enhancing interpretability and
deployment feasibility in resource-limited settings.
• MLDevSecOps framework: The project demonstrates practical realization of MLDe-

6.4
Limitations and Considerations
The work also identifies important limitations that should inform future research and deploy-
ments:
• False positive sensitivity: Under network conditions characterized by unusual but benign
traffic patterns (e.g., bulk data transfers, distributed backups), certain models generate
elevated false positive rates, potentially causing alert fatigue if thresholds are not carefully
calibrated.
• Training data quality: The performance of supervised and semi-supervised models
depends critically on the quality, representativeness, and labeling accuracy of training data.
Imbalanced datasets (far more benign than malicious examples) or label noise can degrade
model performance.
65


===== PAGE 67 =====

• Concept drift and model degradation: As network behavior and attack patterns evolve,
ML models may experience performance degradation over time (concept drift). The
current retraining schedule is static; dynamic triggers based on statistical drift detection
would improve responsiveness.
• Scalability to extreme throughput: While the system achieved 50,000+ events per second
in the lab, production networks with Gbps-scale traffic volumes would require additional
optimizations including GPU acceleration for deep learning inference and distributed
Suricata sensor deployments.
• Adversarial robustness: The evaluation did not explicitly assess robustness against
adversarial attacks (evasion, poisoning) where attackers deliberately attempt to manipulate
or exploit ML models. Hardening against such attacks is an important future direction.

training while preserving data privacy.

geographic distribution, supporting modern cloud-first security operations.
• Active learning and human-in-the-loop optimization: Implementing active learning
strategies could prioritize labeling of the most informative samples, accelerating model
improvement while minimizing analyst effort.

6.6
Recommendations for Practitioners
For organizations considering adoption or deployment of similar MLDevSecOps frameworks,
we offer the following recommendations:
1. Start with clear baselines: Before deploying ML models, establish baseline detection
performance using simple statistical methods and traditional IDS rules to understand the
detection frontier.
2. Invest in data quality: The quality of training data, labeling accuracy, and representative-
ness of attack scenarios directly impact model performance. Allocate significant effort to
data curation and validation.
3. Implement human-in-the-loop feedback: Regular analyst review and labeling of flagged
alerts provides invaluable feedback for model refinement and helps calibrate thresholds to
organizational risk tolerance.
4. Monitor model performance continuously: Deploy production monitoring to track
detection metrics (precision, recall, F1-score) in real time. Automated alerts on metric
degradation enable rapid response to performance issues.

6. Version control everything: Store all pipeline code, configurations, model definitions,

6.7
Final Remarks
This project has demonstrated that integrating network intrusion detection, log analytics, machine
learning, and CI/CD automation into a cohesive MLDevSecOps framework is both technically
feasible and operationally valuable. The resulting system achieved a high degree of detection
accuracy and near-instantaneous response times, demonstrating the viability of lightweight
unsupervised methods in an integrated security pipeline.
While important limitations remain—particularly around false positive sensitivity, scalability
to extreme throughput, and adversarial robustness—the framework provides a solid foundation for
continued research and practical deployment. Its modular architecture, CI/CD-driven automation,
and human-in-the-loop feedback mechanisms position the system well for iterative refinement
and adaptation to evolving threat landscapes.
As machine learning and automation increasingly pervade cybersecurity operations, frame-
works like MLDevSecOps that systematically integrate security, reliability, and operational
practices will become essential. This work offers a practical blueprint for organizations seeking
to harness the power of ML for intelligent, scalable, and trustworthy security operations.
The integration of human expertise with machine learning, supported by transparent and
auditable automation, represents the future direction of cybersecurity. Organizations that success-
fully implement such frameworks will be better equipped to detect and respond to threats with
unprecedented speed and precision, ultimately mitigating risk and alleviating the operational
burden on security teams.
