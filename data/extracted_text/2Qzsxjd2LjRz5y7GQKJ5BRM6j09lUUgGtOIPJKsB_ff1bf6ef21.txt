General Introduction
In an increasingly connected and automated world, the need for intelligent and reliable vehicle
monitoring systems is growing rapidly,especially in environments where visibility is low or
conditions are less than ideal. This project focuses on the development of an automatic license
plate recognition (ALPR) system that operates effectively both during the day, using optical
images, and at night, using thermal imagery.
The core objective is to ensure accurate and consistent recognition of vehicle license plates
regardless of lighting conditions. This is particularly important for surveillance systems, traffic
monitoring, and security applications that must function reliably in real-time, 24/7. A major
challenge addressed in this work is the performance gap between optical and thermal data.
To overcome this, the system integrates mechanisms that help it adapt to differences between
the two image types, with a particular focus on improving recognition in thermal imagery,
where visual information is often limited.
This project was carried out in collaboration with Enova Robotics as part of a professional
internship. Beyond the research and model development phase, it also includes a deployment
phase in which the system is applied to thermal video footage of moving vehicles. This allows
for practical evaluation in a realistic setting and provides insights into the system’s readiness
for real-world use.
This report presents the project’s motivation, goals, technical approach, and evaluation results.
It also discusses the limitations faced and proposes ideas for future improvements to make
the solution more efficient, scalable, and robust for industrial or security deployment.
The dissertation is organized as follows:
• Chapter 1: General context presents the host company, defines the problem ad-
dressed by the project, proposes a solution, and outlines the adopted methodology and
project management approach.

1.2
Review of existing solutions
Most existing ALPR systems are designed for daytime conditions using optical images. They
perform well with modern deep learning models like YOLO for detection and CRNN for

Few approaches use thermal imagery, and even fewer address the gap between optical and
thermal domains. When domain adaptation is used, it’s often basic and not optimized for
real-world deployment.
Critique
• Existing models struggle with thermal images due to lack of training on such data.
• Domain adaptation is rarely applied or not effective enough.
• Most systems are not tested in real-world or video scenarios.
• Recognition models are often not adapted to specific plate formats like Tunisian ones.
1.3
Problem statement
In traditional vehicle monitoring systems, license plate recognition relies heavily on optical
cameras, which perform well during the day but become unreliable in low-light or nighttime
conditions. As a result, vehicles passing at night often go undetected or are misread due to
poor visibility. This limits the effectiveness of surveillance, traffic monitoring, and security
applications. While thermal cameras can operate in the dark, their images lack the details
that most recognition models need. Bridging this gap remains a challenge, especially when
most existing systems are not adapted to handle the differences between optical and thermal
data. These limitations call for a smarter solution that ensures accurate license plate detection
and recognition in all lighting conditions.
1.4
Proposed solution
To overcome the limitations of manual monitoring and ensure reliable performance in all
lighting conditions, we propose developing an automated license plate recognition system that
works with both day and night visuals. The solution aims to detect and read license plates
accurately, even in challenging environments.
This system will reduce dependence on manual inspection, improve consistency, and support
continuous monitoring. By combining visual data with intelligent processing, the solution is

Artificial intelligence
Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to the branch of computer science focused on creating systems

techniques to the more ambitious goal of imitating intelligent human behavior. Modern AI

Machine Learning (ML) is a subset of artificial intelligence (AI) that enables computers to

Domain adaptation
Domain adaptation is a crucial technique in machine learning and computer vision, enabling
models trained on one data distribution (source domain) to perform effectively on a different,
but related, data distribution (target domain). This is particularly important when labeled
data in the target domain is scarce or unavailable[10].

2.7.1
Key definitions
Domain adaptation addresses the challenge of domain shift, where differences in data dis-
tributions between the source and target domains can lead to degraded model performance.
For instance, a model trained on images captured by optical imaging camera may not per-
form well on images taken by thermal imaging camera due to variations in image quality
and characteristics. Domain adaptation techniques aim to bridge this gap by aligning the
feature distributions between domains, allowing the model to generalize better to the target
domain[20][18].
• Source domain: This is the data distribution on which the model is trained using
labeled examples[18].
• Target domain: This is the data distribution on which a model pre-trained on a
different domain is used to perform a similar task[18].
• Domain translation: Domain Translation is the problem of finding a meaningful
correspondence between two domains[18].
• Domain shift: A domain shift is a change in the statistical distribution of data between
the different domains (like the training, validation, and test sets) for a model[18].

2.7.2
Types of domain adaptation
Domain adaptation methods can be categorized based on the availability of labeled data in
the target domain:
• Supervised Domain Adaptation (SDA): Assumes that labeled data is available in
the target domain, facilitating the adaptation process.
• Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA): No labeled data is available in the
target domain. Techniques focus on aligning the feature distributions between the source
and target domains without relying on target labels.
• Semi-supervised domain adaptation: A small amount of labeled data is available in
the target domain, which can be used alongside unlabeled data to improve adaptation.
2.7.3
Techniques in domain adaptation
Several techniques have been developed to facilitate domain adaptation:
• Feature alignment: Aligning the feature distributions of the source and target domains
using methods like Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) or adversarial training
• Adversarial training: Employing domain discriminators to encourage the model to
learn domain-invariant features.
• Domain translation: Transforming data from one domain to resemble the other, often
using generative models like CycleGANs.
• Self-training: Using the model’s predictions on unlabeled target data as pseudo-labels
to iteratively refine the model.
These techniques aim to reduce the discrepancy between domains, enabling the model to
perform effectively on the target domain.

2.8.1
Two stage object detectors
Two-stage object detectors work in two main steps to find and identify objects in an image.
First, they look for parts of the image that might contain an object. This is done by a system
called the Region Proposal Network (RPN). These parts are like guesses of where an object
could be. Then, in the second step, another part of the system takes a closer look at those
guessed areas to figure out exactly what object is there (like a car, a person, or a dog) and to
draw a better box around it.
These types of detectors are very accurate, but because they do two steps, they take a bit
more time than simpler methods. Some of the best-known two-stage object detectors are
R-CNN, Fast R-CNN, Faster R-CNN, and Mask R-CNN.
2.8.1.1
R-CNN (Region-based Convolutional Neural Network)
Introduced in 2014, R-CNN was a pioneering model that combined region proposals with
deep learning for object detection. It utilized selective search to generate approximately 2,000
region proposals per image. Each proposal was then processed through a Convolutional Neural
Network (CNN) to extract features, which were subsequently classified using Support Vector
Machines (SVMs). While R-CNN significantly improved detection accuracy, its computational
inefficiency, due to processing each region independently, posed challenges for real-time
applications[23].
Figure 2.9: Overview of the R-CNN pipeline[23].
2.8.1.2
Fast R-CNN
To address the inefficiencies of R-CNN, Fast R-CNN was developed. This model processed
the entire image through a CNN to produce a feature map, from which region proposals were
extracted using a Region of Interest (RoI) pooling layer. These pooled features were then
fed into fully connected layers for classification and bounding box regression. By sharing

computations across proposals, Fast R-CNN achieved faster training and inference times while
maintaining high accuracy[2][13].
2.8.1.3
Faster R-CNN
Building upon its predecessors, Faster R-CNN introduced the Region Proposal Network
(RPN), a fully convolutional network that generates region proposals directly from the feature
maps. This integration eliminated the need for external proposal methods, enabling end-to-
end training and further improving detection speed and accuracy. Faster R-CNN became a
foundational model in object detection, influencing numerous subsequent architectures[23].
Figure 2.10: Overview of the Faster R-CNN pipeline[23].
2.8.1.4
Mask R-CNN
Extending Faster R-CNN, Mask R-CNN added a parallel branch for predicting segmentation
masks, allowing the model to perform instance segmentation alongside object detection. It
replaced the RoI pooling layer with RoIAlign to preserve spatial alignment, enhancing the
quality of the predicted masks. Mask R-CNN demonstrated versatility in tasks requiring
precise object delineation, such as medical imaging and autonomous driving[23].

2.8.2
One stage object detectors
One-stage detectors predict both the class (what the object is) and the exact position of the
object at the same time. Since they do everything in a single step, they are much faster.
That’s why they are widely used in real-time object detection. These detectors focus on speed
and are very fast, but they may be slightly less accurate when detecting oddly shaped objects
or small objects that are close together. Some of the most well-known one-stage detectors are:
YOLO, SSD and RetinaNet.
2.8.2.1
SSD (Multibox Single Shot Detector)
The Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD) is an object detection algorithm that employs a
single convolutional neural network (CNN) to simultaneously predict class probabilities and
bounding box coordinates for a set of default boxes. These default boxes, also known as
anchor boxes, are predefined with various aspect ratios and scales, enabling the detection
of objects of different sizes and shapes. The CNN architecture typically comprises a base
network, such as VGG or ResNet, followed by additional convolutional and pooling layers
that generate predictions for class probabilities and bounding box coordinates.

YOLO (You Only Look Once)
YOLO, short for "You Only Look Once," is a real-time object detection system that reframes

an S × S grid, and each grid cell predicts B bounding boxes, confidence scores, and class
probabilities. This unified approach enables YOLO to achieve high detection speeds while
maintaining accuracy[30].

2.9.1
CRNN (Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network)
CRNN is a hybrid architecture that combines Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) to effectively recognize text in images.
• Convolutional Layers (CNNs): These layers extract spatial features from the input
image, capturing patterns like edges and textures.
• Recurrent Layers (RNNs): The extracted features are then passed through RNNs,
typically using Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units, to model the sequential nature
of text.
• Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC): CTC is employed as the loss function
to align the predicted sequences with the target text without requiring pre-segmented
data.
CRNNs are particularly effective for recognizing regular, horizontally aligned text, such as in
license plates or printed documents. However, they may struggle with irregular text layouts
or complex backgrounds.
2.9.2
Attention-based encoder-decoder models
These models enhance the traditional encoder-decoder architecture by incorporating an
attention mechanism, allowing the model to focus on specific parts of the input image when
generating each character in the output sequence.
• Encoder: Typically a CNN that processes the input image to extract feature representa-
tions.
• Attention mechanism: This component assigns weights to different parts of the encoded
features, guiding the decoder to focus on relevant regions for each output step.
• Decoder: Often an RNN or Transformer that generates the output text sequence, one
character at a time, using the context provided by the attention mechanism.
• Attention-based models are well-suited for recognizing text in complex scenes, curved
layouts, or images with varying orientations. They offer improved accuracy over models
without attention mechanisms but can be more computationally intensive.

Transformer-based models
Transformer-based models, leverage the Transformer architecture’s self-attention mechanisms

The Faster R-CNN architecture follows a two-stage detection approach. It processes input
images through several key components as follows:

– The classifier assigns a class label to the proposed region ("license plate").
– The regressor adjusts the bounding box coordinates to better fit the actual object.
• Output: The final output consists of:
– Refined bounding boxes that localize objects within the image.
– Corresponding class labels for each detected object.

The primary objective of this project was to mitigate the domain shift between optical (source)

4.2.1.1
Used method
To address the domain shift, a Domain-Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) was integrated
into the YOLOv8 framework. The DANN approach facilitates unsupervised domain adaptation
by adversarially aligning feature distributions across the optical and thermal domains, enabling
the model to focus on domain-invariant features critical for license plate detection. This
method was selected for its proven efficacy in handling domain discrepancies without requiring
labeled target data.

modifications, and built-in training utilities, which facilitated the integration of the Domain-
Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) framework.
Training hyperparameters
• Epochs: 70, to ensure convergence of detection and domain adaptation objectives.
This was increased from the initially tested 50 epochs to allow for more robust feature
alignment.
• Batch size: 16, with equal representation of optical and thermal images per batch.
• Learning rate: 0.001 using the AdamW optimizer with a cosine annealing schedule.
A separate RMSprop optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10−4 was used for the
discriminator, warmed up over the first 100 steps.
Dataset
• Source domain (Optical): Labeled images, serving as the supervised training set.
• Target domain (Thermal): Unlabeled thermal images (351 images in the validation
set), with pseudo-labels generated during training.

4.4
OCR implementation with domain adaptation
To address the performance gap between optical and thermal data, we applied domain
adaptation techniques. The next sections outline the baseline results and the improvements
achieved through this approach.
4.4.1
Baseline evaluation on optical and thermal
The OCR model, initially trained on optical data, achieved high performance on the optical
validation set (1,310 samples), with a best accuracy of 94.809% at iteration 14,000, as reported
in the section above. The model was then evaluated on the thermal validation set (377
samples) without domain adaptation, using the pre-trained weights from the trained model.
The accuracy on thermal data was 29.178%, The significant performance gap between optical
and thermal domains highlights the domain shift, as thermal images lack the texture and
contrast of optical images, making character recognition more challenging.
4.4.2
Domain adaptation strategy for OCR
To address the domain shift between optical and thermal license plate images, a second training
phase was conducted using the deep text recognition benchmark framework, integrating domain
adaptation techniques:
• Batch composition: A total batch size of 127 was used during training.
• Domain-adversarial loss: A lightweight neural network (domain classifier) was added
to distinguish whether the extracted features came from optical or thermal images.
It predicted values close to 1 for optical and 0 for thermal inputs. A binary cross-
entropy loss was used, weighted by domain_lambda = 0.7. This adversarial setup
encouraged the model to learn domain-invariant features, making optical and thermal
data representations more similar and reducing the domain gap.
• Pseudo-labeling: the model generated pseudo-labels using a confidence threshold of
0.5
• Training settings: The model was trained for 20,000 iterations with an initial learning
rate of 0.001 using the Adam optimizer. and validation was performed every 1,000
iterations using the thermal validation set.

domain.
