General Introduction
In recent years, information systems have become increasingly complex as organizations
adopt cloud based architectures, distributed applications, microservices and a growing
ecosystem of interconnected devices. This expansion has led to a significant rise in the
volume of operational data (logs, metrics, traces and alerts), placing growing pressure on
monitoring and security operations teams. In highly regulated sectors such as banking, ef-
fective observability and cybersecurity practices are essential to ensure service availability,
protect sensitive data, meet regulatory requirements and enable fast incident detection
and response. According to the IBM Cost of a Data Breach Report 2024, the financial
sector ranks among the highest cost industries for security incidents for the thirteenth
consecutive year, with an average breach cost of $6.08 million and organizations without
advanced tooling face an average of 194 days to identify a breach and 64 days to contain
it [39].
While SIEM and monitoring platforms provide indispensable capabilities for aggregation,
correlation and alerting, their effective use frequently requires advanced technical ex-
pertise, familiarity with query languages and significant manual effort to create rules and
interpret results. These requirements create operational friction that may delay triage and
remediation during incidents. In practice, alert fatigue is a major operational challenge:
industry surveys consistently identify false positives and unmanageable alert volumes as
the leading challenges for SOC teams, with a significant proportion of daily alerts left
uninvestigated due to analyst capacity constraints [40].
This report presents LOGI, a locally hosted intelligent assistant designed to reduce the
gap between human analysts and complex observability systems. LOGI enables users to
ask natural language questions about infrastructure and security events. It automatically
routes those queries to the appropriate backend systems: Graylog for log retrieval and
search, Zabbix for metrics and availability checks and a retrieval augmented generation
(RAG) knowledge base for contextual and procedural information. All AI inference and
language model processing are executed locally using Ollama, thereby preserving data
privacy, minimizing exposure to external providers and addressing concerns related to
vendor lock in, rising cloud costs and regulatory compliance.
1


===== PAGE 15 =====

The primary objectives of this work are:
• To design and implement a prototype capable of mapping natural language queries
to the appropriate operational data sources and returning evidence based analytical
responses.
• To demonstrate that local LLM inference can be effectively integrated with exist-
ing observability platforms without transmitting sensitive operational data to third
party cloud services.
• To evaluate the implemented system in terms of routing effectiveness, answer rele-
vance, execution latency and operational usability.
Target users for LOGI include SOC analysts, incident responders and platform engineers
operating within sensitive environments where data privacy and regulatory constraints
limit the adoption of cloud hosted AI solutions. The use cases explored in this work
include rapid incident investigation through correlated logs and metrics, guided forensic
analysis for security events and contextual operational knowledge assistance.
Methodologically, the project follows the Scrum agile methodology, organized into a
preparatory phase followed by two implementation sprints over a six month timeline. The
preparatory phase focused on establishing a mirrored infrastructure environment replicat-
ing the target banking operational ecosystem. Sprint 1 focused on implementing the core
platform architecture, including the web interface, FastAPI backend, intelligent routing
engine and associated validation activities. Sprint 2 focused on integrating the operational
analytical backends, including Graylog, Zabbix and the RAG knowledge pipeline, along
with the implementation and validation of the corresponding analytical workflows.
This report contributes an applied architecture and implementation that combines local
LLM inference with existing observability tools to provide secure, private and explainable
natural language access to operational data.

1.4
Problem Statement
The main problem addressed by this project is the difficulty of efficiently interpreting
and making sense of massive volumes of log and monitoring data in highly regulated
environments while avoiding dependence on cloud-based AI services.
Traditional logging and monitoring tools generate large numbers of events and alerts, but
they do not provide a natural language interface that allows analysts to query, summarize
and understand what is happening in the infrastructure. As a result, operators are often
required to manually correlate logs, metrics, alerts and inventory data.
At the same time, relying on cloud-based AI providers for log interpretation may introduce
privacy, compliance, cost and dependency concerns. In sensitive environments such as
banking infrastructures, operational logs may contain confidential information that must
remain within internal systems.
Therefore, this project addresses the challenge of building a local, on-premises AI assistant
capable of enabling natural language log interpretation and operational insight while
ensuring full control over data residency, infrastructure and cost structure.

1.8
Proposed Solution
In response to the identified limitations of existing monitoring and AI-assisted security
solutions, the LOGI project proposes a secure, local and intelligent analytical platform de-
signed for infrastructure monitoring and security investigation in regulated environments.
LOGI is not intended to replace existing operational tools, but rather to enhance them
through an intelligent AI interaction layer.
The platform integrates multiple comple-
mentary systems, including Graylog for centralized log analysis, Zabbix for infrastructure
monitoring and a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) knowledge base for contextual
technical assistance.
Through a natural language interface, users can express operational or security-related
requests in human-readable form without requiring direct knowledge of complex query
languages or platform-specific commands.
LOGI intelligently interprets each request,
routes it to the appropriate backend systems, coordinates data retrieval and generates
explainable analytical responses.
All artificial intelligence inference is performed locally using open-source language models,
ensuring full control over data privacy, operational confidentiality and deployment inde-
pendence. This architecture makes the proposed solution particularly suitable for highly
regulated sectors such as banking, where sensitive operational information must remain
within internal infrastructure boundaries.
By combining multi-source orchestration, local AI inference, natural language interac-
tion and explainable analytical assistance, LOGI aims to improve investigation efficiency,
reduce manual analysis effort and enhance operational decision-making.

• Enable interaction with multiple backend systems, including Graylog, Zabbix and

• Support secure query generation and backend request validation.
• Aggregate responses from multiple operational sources into a coherent analytical

• Preserve full local control over operational data and AI inference.
• Maintain modularity to support future architectural evolution.
In addition to these functional objectives, the architecture must satisfy several technical

The main identified constraints are:

2.6.2
Request Processing Strategy
The LOGI platform adopts a modular request processing strategy designed to transform
natural language user requests into structured operational workflows.
Since the platform must interact with multiple backend systems, a direct one-model con-
versational architecture would be insufficient. Instead, LOGI relies on an orchestration-
based processing strategy that separates request understanding, routing, backend inter-
action, evidence retrieval and response synthesis.
When a stakeholder submits a request through the web interface, the request is first
transmitted to the FastAPI backend through the application API layer. The backend
then forwards the request to the intelligent processing engine responsible for request in-
terpretation and orchestration.
The request processing strategy is organized into several stages:
• Request reception: capture of the natural language request from the web inter-
face.
• Intent analysis: identification of the operational objective of the request.
• Routing decision: determination of the backend services required to satisfy the
request.
• Backend query execution: interaction with the selected operational systems.
• Response aggregation: consolidation of retrieved operational information.
• AI-assisted synthesis: generation of a structured and explainable response.
This architecture allows the platform to handle both simple single-source requests and
more complex analytical requests requiring simultaneous interaction with multiple back-
end systems.
For example:
25


===== PAGE 39 =====

• A security investigation request may be routed toward Graylog.
• A performance monitoring request may target Zabbix.
• A contextual documentation request may use the RAG knowledge base.
• A composite operational investigation may require coordinated interaction between
multiple services.
This modular orchestration strategy improves flexibility, maintainability, explainability
and operational scalability compared to monolithic conversational architectures.
2.6.3
User Request Processing Workflow
To ensure structured handling of analytical requests, the LOGI platform follows a multi-
stage processing workflow that transforms user questions into explainable operational
responses.
The workflow begins when a stakeholder submits a natural language request through the
platform interface. The request is transmitted to the backend API, where it is forwarded
to the intelligent processing engine responsible for interpretation and orchestration.
The request is first analyzed in order to determine its operational intent. Based on this
interpretation, the routing mechanism identifies the backend services required to satisfy
the request.
Depending on the nature of the query, the system may interact with:
• Graylog for log analysis and security event retrieval.
• Zabbix for infrastructure monitoring metrics and alert information.
• The RAG knowledge base for contextual documentation retrieval.
Retrieved information is then consolidated by the response aggregation layer, which orga-
nizes operational evidence before forwarding the structured context to the local language
model.
Finally, the AI synthesis layer generates an explainable analytical response presented to
the stakeholder through the interface.

2.6.4
AI and Multi-System Architecture
The LOGI architecture is based on a modular multi-system design. Instead of relying
on a single monolithic assistant, the platform separates user interaction, request rout-
ing, backend communication, evidence retrieval and response generation into independent
components.
The stakeholder interacts with the system through a web interface served by a FastAPI
backend. Each request is sent to a unified processing layer, which analyzes the question
and determines the appropriate target systems. According to the detected intent, the
request may be routed to Graylog, Zabbix, the RAG knowledge base or multiple systems
simultaneously.
The Graylog pipeline is responsible for security log retrieval and analysis. It generates
safe query plans, validates them, compiles them into Graylog-compatible queries, retrieves
log evidence and produces security-oriented interpretations.
27


===== PAGE 41 =====

The Zabbix pipeline handles infrastructure monitoring requests. It generates safe read-
only SQL queries, validates them, retrieves metrics or alert information, extracts relevant
observations and produces monitoring-oriented analysis.
The RAG pipeline retrieves contextual knowledge from embedded documents stored in a
vector database. This allows the assistant to answer documentation-related or inventory-
related questions using retrieved evidence instead of relying only on the internal knowledge
of the language model.
All generated responses are consolidated by the response aggregation layer. This layer
organizes the outputs from different systems into separate sections and prepares the final
answer presented to the stakeholder.
Local language model inference is performed through Ollama, which allows LOGI to oper-
ate without depending on external cloud AI services. This design supports confidentiality,
local control and adaptability to sensitive infrastructure environments.

2.6.5
Dual-Model Processing Architecture
A deliberate architectural constraint governs every analytical pipeline in LOGI: no single
language model invocation is permitted to both generate an executable query and analyze
29


===== PAGE 43 =====

the data returned by that query. Instead, each pipeline splits its language model usage
into two strictly isolated roles, referred to throughout this report as Model A and Model
B.
Model A – Query Generator. Model A receives only the stakeholder’s natural lan-
guage question and the relevant schema or field documentation.
Its sole output is a
structured executable artifact, such as a JSON QueryPlan for the Graylog pipeline or a
SELECT SQL statement for the Zabbix pipeline. It is invoked with deterministic settings
to ensure that its output remains machine-parseable and reproducible. Critically, Model
A is called before any backend data is retrieved; it never receives log messages, metric
rows or any other operational content.
Model B – Analyst.
Model B receives the original question together with a pre-
aggregated and structured representation of the retrieved evidence, such as statistical
features extracted from log messages or normalized metric objects produced by the mon-
itoring analysis layer. It never constructs or modifies queries. Its output is a human-
readable analytical report.
This separation provides three important properties:
• Prompt injection isolation: because Model A completes before backend data is
fetched, a malicious string embedded in a log message or metric item name cannot
influence the query generation process.
• Query determinism: deterministic Model A configuration makes query generation
auditable and reproducible.
• Evidence-based analysis: Model B analyzes only retrieved and structured evi-
dence, which limits unsupported conclusions and improves traceability.

scalability, and explainability.
The architecture separates user interaction, backend orchestration, operational data ac-
cess, and artificial intelligence processing into distinct layers. This separation simplifies
maintenance, facilitates future extensions and reduces coupling between system compo-
nents.
The architecture is organized into four principal layers:
• Presentation Layer: responsible for user interaction through the web interface.
This layer captures natural language requests and presents analytical responses.

response aggregation. This layer contains the FastAPI backend and intelligent con-
trol logic.
• Operational Data Layer: responsible for providing infrastructure and contextual
information through Graylog, Zabbix and the RAG knowledge base.
• Artificial Intelligence Layer: responsible for language model inference, semantic
retrieval and explainable analytical response generation.

RAG Environment Preparation

tions of textual content, while ChromaDB was used as the vector database responsible
for storing and retrieving embedded knowledge fragments.
LangChain was used as the orchestration layer to facilitate document ingestion, embedding
generation and retrieval execution.
The RAG environment was configured to support contextual responses related to infras-
tructure documentation, VM inventory information and technical operational knowledge
required by the assistant.

engine responsible for dynamically determining the most appropriate backend system for
each analytical request.

analysis, infrastructure monitoring, and contextual knowledge retrieval, a static request
handling strategy would be insufficient.
Instead, an intelligent routing mechanism was implemented to analyze the semantics of
incoming user requests and determine the most relevant execution path.

which backend system should process a given analytical request.
By introducing automated request classification, the platform improves usability, reduces
operational complexity and enables unified interaction across heterogeneous backend sys-
tems.
This design allows analysts to interact with the system through natural language rather
than system-specific technical interfaces.

Security and Robustness Mechanisms
Since the LOGI platform interacts with operational monitoring and log analysis systems,
several security and robustness mechanisms were implemented during Sprint 1 to ensure
controlled usage and stable execution.
4.7.1
Request Rate Limiting
The backend implements a rate limiting mechanism to restrict the number of requests
submitted by the same client within a defined time window.
In the implemented version, each client is limited to a maximum of ten requests per sixty
seconds. This mechanism reduces the risk of abuse, excessive backend load or accidental
request flooding.
4.7.2
Timeout Handling
To prevent long-running requests from blocking backend execution, timeout handling was
implemented around analytical pipeline calls.
If a request exceeds the configured execution timeout, the backend returns a controlled
timeout response instead of leaving the user waiting indefinitely. This improves platform
stability and provides predictable error handling.
4.7.3
Audit Logging
The platform records analytical interactions in a local JSON Lines (JSONL) audit log file.
Each interaction is stored as a structured JSON entry containing relevant execution meta-
data, including the timestamp, session identifier, selected execution mode, user request,
execution duration and processing status.
This lightweight file-based logging mechanism provides persistent traceability while re-
maining simple to append, inspect and maintain within the prototype environment.
The audit mechanism improves traceability and supports debugging, monitoring and op-
erational review activities.
4.7.4
Backend Availability Checks
The /api/status endpoint verifies the operational availability of the integrated systems,

5.5
Dual-Model Architecture
Before describing the individual backend pipelines, it is important to introduce an archi-
tectural principle shared by both the Graylog and Zabbix integrations: the dual-model
design.
Each analytical pipeline is divided into two strictly separated language model invocations,
referred to as Model A and Model B, each assigned a distinct and non-overlapping role.
• Model A — Query Generator: receives the user’s natural language question and
produces a structured executable artifact (a Graylog query plan or a SQL query for
Zabbix). It is configured deterministically to guarantee structured and parseable
output.
• Model B — Analyst Reasoning Model: receives structured extracted evidence
derived from backend results and generates the final analytical report presented to
the user.
This architectural separation improves modularity, reduces hallucination risks, strength-
ens security controls, and ensures clearer separation between backend execution and an-
alytical reasoning.

Between raw metrics retrieval and language model interpretation, the Zabbix analytical
pipeline integrates an intermediate deterministic observability analysis subsystem referred
to as the ObservabilityBrain.
Unlike purely LLM-driven analytical architectures, this subsystem introduces a structured
rule-based reasoning layer capable of interpreting infrastructure metrics before they are
passed to the language model.
The objective of this architectural layer is to improve analytical consistency, reduce hal-
lucination risks, and provide explainable deterministic reasoning for infrastructure moni-
toring scenarios.

The ObservabilityBrain operates as a specialized analytical engine responsible for trans-
forming raw monitoring data into structured operational evidence.
Its internal architecture is composed of four principal components:
• Interpreter: responsible for normalizing and structuring retrieved monitoring met-
rics into a consistent internal representation suitable for analysis.
• Analyzer: responsible for evaluating metric values against predefined operational
rules and thresholds in order to identify abnormal infrastructure conditions.
• Correlator: responsible for establishing relationships between multiple observa-
tions, allowing broader operational patterns and infrastructure anomalies to be de-
tected.
• Formatter: responsible for organizing deterministic analytical findings into struc-
tured evidence consumable by the final language model.
Through this layered deterministic reasoning workflow, the ObservabilityBrain produces
pre-analyzed infrastructure insights that complement probabilistic language model rea-
soning.
This hybrid architecture ensures that the final analytical response benefits from both
deterministic observability expertise and natural language analytical interpretation.

General Conclusion
The increasing complexity of modern IT infrastructures and the growing volume of oper-
ational logs have made infrastructure monitoring and security analysis increasingly chal-
lenging tasks. Traditional investigation workflows often require direct interaction with
multiple heterogeneous platforms, technical expertise in backend-specific query mecha-
nisms and significant manual effort to correlate operational information across different
sources.
In this context, the objective of this end-of-studies project was to design and implement
LOGI, an intelligent analytical assistant capable of simplifying infrastructure monitoring
and security investigations through natural language interaction.
The proposed solution was designed around a local artificial intelligence architecture in-
tegrating multiple operational systems, including Graylog for centralized log analysis,
Zabbix for infrastructure monitoring and a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) en-
vironment for contextual knowledge retrieval.
To achieve this objective, the project was conducted using the Scrum agile methodology,
enabling iterative development across multiple implementation sprints.
The first implementation phase focused on preparing a mirrored infrastructure environ-
ment representative of the target operational ecosystem. This included the deployment
of Graylog, Zabbix, PostgreSQL, Ollama and the contextual retrieval infrastructure, es-
tablishing the technical foundation required for development.
The second phase focused on implementing the core software architecture, including the
web-based user interface, FastAPI backend, intelligent routing engine, security mecha-
nisms and request orchestration workflows.
The third phase introduced backend analytical integrations, enabling coordinated interac-
tion with operational monitoring systems and the implementation of analytical pipelines
for Graylog, Zabbix and RAG.
Finally, the validation phase confirmed the operational correctness of the implemented
system through structured functional testing across all major subsystems.
The obtained results demonstrate that the proposed architecture successfully enables
unified natural language interaction across heterogeneous operational backends while pre-
89


===== PAGE 103 =====

serving modularity, local execution and analytical flexibility.
One of the most significant contributions of this work lies in the implementation of intelli-
gent multi-source analytical orchestration, allowing coordinated investigations across log
analysis, monitoring systems and contextual knowledge sources through a single interface.
Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. The current implementation has not yet under-
gone formal large-scale performance benchmarking and some analytical behaviors remain
partially dependent on language model response consistency. In addition, deployment val-
idation was performed in a mirrored development environment rather than a production
enterprise infrastructure.
Despite these limitations, the implemented solution successfully demonstrates the practi-
cal feasibility of a local AI-powered analytical assistant for infrastructure monitoring and
security investigation.
Future Work
Several improvement directions may be considered for future evolution of the LOGI plat-
form:
• implementation of formal quantitative performance benchmarking
• deployment validation in production-scale enterprise environments
• support for additional backend analytical systems and SIEM platforms
• implementation of advanced evidence correlation mechanisms
• enhanced conversational memory for long-running investigations
• fine-tuning of language models for cybersecurity-specific analytical tasks
• implementation of role-based access control and enterprise authentication integra-
tion
• dashboard-based analytical visualization enhancements.
In conclusion, this project demonstrates the potential of combining local artificial intel-
ligence, infrastructure monitoring platforms and intelligent orchestration mechanisms to
modernize operational analytical workflows in cybersecurity and IT infrastructure man-
agement.
