Literature Review
LN is a frequent and serious complication of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), a

It is marked by inflammation of the kidneys, that may lead to
progressive renal damage and, without intervention, irreversible renal failure: End-Stage Renal

including tissue fixation and specific staining techniques including Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E),

tissue samples by microscopy to identify glomerular structures and assign classes according to the
ISN/RPS system.

pathologists are required to manually inspect microscopic images for disease severity identification.
This manual interpretation is time-consuming and highly dependent on specialist expertise.
According to a research by Garg et al.[1], patients who have been diagnosed with lupus
nephritis face a 26-times higher mortality rate compared to their peers, also the average waiting
time for diagnostic could exceed about 50 days even in well-resourced healthcare systems.

how consistently Korean renal pathologist classified LN from identical digital images, reported
only moderate agreement among pathologists proving that manual interpretation is also limited

1.3.4
Proposed Solution
To help resolve the issues, discussed previously, regarding LN diagnosis in the Tunisian
healthcare system, we propose an AI-assisted diagnostic tool. This solution seeks to mitigate the
burden of manual kidney biopsy analysis process performed at CHU Hedi Chaker Sfax, as well as to
increase the efficiency, reliability, and nationwide accessibility of LN diagnosis. The proposed solution
consists of a multi-stage deep learning pipeline that processes stained kidney biopsy specimens
captured through light microscopy using a digital camera. This system aims to aid pathologists to
assess and categorize the glomerular structures according to ISN/RPS classification system (Classes
I-VI). The pipeline consists of three core components:
• Glomeruli Detection: The system first processes the biopsy images to detect the glomeruli
and their positions to aid in the selection of the structures for the next stage.
• Glomeruli Segmentation: This stage improves the accuracy of the glomeruli borders by
providing exact depiction of their shapes.
• Glomeruli Classification: Finally, categorization by severity similiar to the ISN/RPS class
is done on the segmented glomeruli to aid in further diagnostic decision.
To address domain-specific challenges, such as incomplete glomeruli at the borders of images
or the recurrence of the same glomerulus in several biopsy photographs, the system automates
preprocessing and filtering tasks. This ensures that only the pertinent glomerular instances are
preserved and analyzed, safeguarding the diagnostic accuracy.
The solution offers a clinician-oriented interface that fits into the clinical workflows. With
this interface, the nephrologists and pathologists are enabled to upload the biopsy images, see the
results of automation (for example, the annotated glomeruli with their classification and labels),
adjust them manually as needed, and produce diagnostic reports. The system also embeds privacy-preserving
techniques like data anonymization to comply with clinical data security requirements.

2.2
Clinical Background of Lupus Nephritis
2.2.1
Disease Definition
Lupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most serious and common presentations of systemic
lupus erythematosus (SLE), an autoimmune disease marked by the production of autoantibodies
against nuclear antigens and the formation of immune complexes that get deposited in various
organs. Among these, the kidneys are highly vulnerable, and immune-mediated injury results in the
inflammation of the glomerular, followed by progressive fibrosis and eventual end-stage renal disease
(ESRD) in extreme cases [4].
2.2.2
Epidemiology
Lupus nephritis (LN) is a severe and common form of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
worldwide, affecting approximately 60% of patients with SLE [4] and is a critical cause of morbidity
and mortality [5]. Regional data reveal prevalence rates for LN in Tunisia, particularly in children.
A multicenter retrospective study in 40 Tunisian children with biopsy-proven LN revealed 27.5%
developed end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and 17.5% died; a composite end-point (ESRD or death)
happened in 45% patients [6]. Predictors for unfavorable renal outcome found in the study were age
13


===== PAGE 29 =====

Chapter 2. Literature Review
at disease onset above 14 years, raised serum creatinine, and histopathological features such as wire
loops and fibrous crescents. These findings further highlight Tunisian patients’ high clinical disease
burden and prompt proper diagnosis as a route towards improved patient outcomes.
2.2.3
Pathophysiology
LN is caused by immune-mediated systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). Immune complexes
formed by autoantibodies are deposited in glomeruli and activate the complement system leading
to an inflammatory cascade. Mesangial proliferation, endocapillary proliferation, endothelial injury,
and infiltration by immune cells are manifestations of inflammation. Slowly, chronic inflammation
leads to sclerosis in glomeruli and interstitial fibrosis, which may advance to end-stage renal disease
(ESRD) if untreated [4]. Understanding such mechanisms is critical for artificial intelligence-based
diagnostic approaches because mild structural variations in glomeruli are an indicator of disease
activity as well as severity. Accurate determination of such variations is useful for early intervention
for better patient outcomes.
2.2.4
Histopathological Classification
ISN/RPS classification categorizes LN into six histological classes based on glomerular
involvement.
Classes III (focal proliferative) and IV (diffuse proliferative) are most severe and
clinically significant and are indicative of active inflammatory disease with a high risk for subsequent
renal impairment.
• Class III (Focal Proliferative LN): Less than 50% of severe glomeruli are involved. Characterized
by segmental endocapillary proliferation, an occasional crescent, and immune complex deposits.
• Class IV (Diffuse Proliferative LN): More than 50% of severe glomeruli are involved.
Characterized by diffuse endocapillary proliferation, wide crescents, and wide immune complex
deposits, it is the class with the worst prognosis in LN classes.
It aligns with our AI project, which is a severity-based classification of glomeruli. Automated
identification of Class IV and Class III lesions can assist pathologists diagnosing in high-risk cases,
reducing diagnostic variability, and taking more appropriate treatment decisions.

2.2.5
Current Diagnostic Workflow
Renal biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing and staging lupus nephritis (LN).
It provides a direct assessment of glomerular involvement so that pathologists can determine disease
activity, chronicity, and severity. Kidney biopsy in clinical practice is typically performed percutaneously
under ultrasound guidance by using a core needle so that appropriate cortical tissue is obtained for
histopathological examination 2.1.
Once it is recovered, the biopsy specimen is prepared for a number of stains including
hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid-Schiff (PAS), silver, and trichrome staining which show
distinctive histological features. Immunofluorescence is also used in a few cases for immune complex
deposition diagnosis.
An experienced nephropathologist then classifies the LN by means of the
ISN/RPS classification system with particular attention to Class III (focal proliferative) and Class
IV (diffuse proliferative), which are most severe as well as clinically significant forms.
Whereas biopsy interpretation remains definitive diagnosis, it is slow and subject to inter-observer
variation. Subtle variations in morphology at the glomerular level lead to inconsistent grading of
disease activity and severity within a biopsy section, which can affect decisions about therapy. These
drawbacks make a potential application for an AI-augmented method: glomeruli are identified and
stratified in biopsy photographs without human intervention in order to improve consistency, reduce
diagnostic delay, and provide quantitative estimation of disease burden.

algorithms, and architectures underlying most of the modern AI systems is presented with particular
reference to machine learning and deep learning models.
2.3.1
Artificial Intelligence
Artificial Intelligence is a computer paradigm that seeks to design systems that can execute
tasks that necessarily involve human intelligence. Such tasks involve pattern recognition, decision
making, problem solving, learning, and reasoning. Systems of AI execute through algorithms in
computing systems and manipulate data in order to glean useful information and make predications
or choices. The field of AI has numerous approaches, ranging from symbolic reasoning and expert
systems to statistical learning methods. Cutting-edge AIs consist primarily of data-driven approaches
that learn from large collections of data and are not specifically programmed with rules. This shift
from rule-based systems to learning systems has enabled AI to address increasingly complex problems
in many different areas. AI can be classified into narrow AI (developed for a limited set of tasks) and
general AI (hypothetical systems with human-level cognitive capacities in all areas). State-of-the-art
useful applications mainly come under narrow AI, where systems do very well in a limited set of
tasks using task-specialized algorithms and architectures.
2.3.2
Machine Learning
Machine Learning is a branch of artificial intelligence that allows computer systems to learn
and improve automatically and enhance performance on a certain task through experience, and
without explicit programming for every scenario. The basic concept behind ML is the capability of
recognizing patterns in data and employing such patterns in order to make a decision or a prediction
on new, unseen data.

2.3.3
Deep Learning
Deep Learning is a subfield of machine learning that uses artificial neural networks with
multiple processing layers to deal with complicated data structures. The term "deep" is used to
denote the many processing layers that allow these networks to discover hierarchical representations
of data, extracting automatically more abstract features with each level. Artificial Neural Networks
are the basic architecture in deep learning frameworks.

2.3.3.1
Artificial Neural Networks (ANN)
Artificial neural networks are composed of processing nodes or neurons that are connected
in order to form layers that process data through weighted connections as well as through nonlinear
activation functions. A basic neural network has three primary parts:

2.3.3.1.1
Network Architecture
• Input Layer: Takes raw data as input and forwards it to the following layers. All The neurons
in this layer represent one dimension or feature in the input data.
• Hidden Layers: Perform intermediate computation from weighted connections and activation
functions. Deep networks have many hidden layers, allowing for complex pattern recognition
and feature extraction.
• Output Layer: This layer is used to produce the final output predictions or classifications
using the flattened data from hidden layers. The number of neurons depends on the type of
task (a single neuron for binary classification, and multiple neurons for multi-class problems).
2.3.3.1.2
Learning Process
Neural networks are trained through backpropagation, a multi-step process, as follows:
1. Forward Propagation: First, the input values are propagated through the network layer by
layer where transformations using weights and activation functions are done.
2. Loss Calculation: We compare the network output with the desired output with a loss
function suitable for the task (cross-entropy for classification and mean squared error for
regression).
3. Back propagation: Error gradients are back-propagated through the network using the chain
rule to calculate the contribution of every weight to the error.
4. Updating Parameters: Gradient descent optimisation algorithm updates network weights
and bias terms to minimise the loss function.
5. Iteration: This process is repeated throughout many epochs until convergence or satisfying
performance is achieved.

2.3.3.2.1
Network Architecture
• Convolutional Layer: The most significant building block in CNN is the convolutional layer,

applying the convolutional operation, utilizing a group of filters that are same in size, each one
that slides through the input image and produces the feature map.
• Pooling Layer: It is designed to reduce the spatial dimensions of the convoluted image in

computational cost when training.
• Fully Connected Layer: The fully connected layer performs the same duties found in
standard ANNs, and takes in the output in the prior layer that is flattened and produces
the final prediction as class probability or regression output.

Advanced Techniques

• Batch Normalization: This technique standardizesthe inputs of each layer, Accelerating

layers, this enables the training of deep architectures by mitigating the problem of vanishing
gradient.

Supervised Learning

Unsupervised Learning (Brief Overview)
The algorithm operates on data without predefined labels, by identifying correlations and

Transfer learning involves applying knowledge from pre-trained large-dataset models to
obtain performance on similar tasks with small samples. Unlike from-scratch model training, transfer
learning adjusts available representations to new problems or environments while significantly reducing
computational cost as well as extensive training time requirement.

• Domain Adaptation: Transferring models learned in one domain to generalize effectively in
another domain that is related.

can reach very high performance in situations where we are data-limited. In this project, it is most

2.3.5
Transformers
The Transformer model has transformed natural language processing as we know it and has
gained popularity in computer vision and other fields. Transformers use self-attention to discover
long-distance dependencies and associations in data sequences.
2.3.5.1
Core Architecture
• Self-Attention Mechanism: Enables models to give weights to the importance of different
regions in the input while looking into each item. The mechanism calculates attention between
all item pairs in a sequence such that the model can focus on significant information regardless
of positional proximity.
• Multi-Head Attention: Several attention mechanisms used in parallel to one another, each
inspecting different types of relationships in the information. The output is catenated and
linearly transformed to provide final attention outputs.
• Positional Encoding: Since Transformers process sequences in parallel, not sequentially,
positional encodings provide information on the positions of the elements in the sequences.
• Feed-Forward Networks: Apply attention outputs through fully connected, nonlinearly
activated layers to offer capacity for complex transformations.
2.3.5.2
Benefits and Applications
Transformers are very good at preserving long-range dependents, parallelizing computations
effectively, and dealing with sequences of any length. Transformers have obtained state-of-the-art

Vision Transformers (ViTs): Apply the Transformer architecture to image analysis by
considering image patches as sequences. Though computationally expensive, ViTs have achieved
state-of-the-art performance comparable to CNNs on many vision tasks, especially when large
datasets are employed for their training.
2.3.6
Foundation Models
Foundation models are large pre-trained models trained from heterogeneous datasets that
can be fine-tuned to downstream tasks. Foundation models learn general representations that can
be transferred to diverse application environments.
2.3.6.1
Characteristics
Foundation models are distinguished by their historical scale, being trained on huge datasets
with billions of parameters. They are exceptionally general, succeeding in a wide variety of tasks
without task-specificity in their training. Fine-tuning for task-specific application is enabled while
taking advantage of learned knowledge broadly.
2.3.6.2
Examples and Impact
Natural language processing was revolutionized with language models like GPT and BERT,
while vision-language models like CLIP bridge text and image understanding. Such models have
introduced new abilities in zero-shot learning, in which models carry out tasks without exact
instances for training.

2.4.2
Limitations and Research Opportunities
Despite promising advances in deep learning approaches for lupus nephritis detection based
on renal biopsies, critical limitations reveal significant research opportunities. Most existing works
rely on conventional CNN based architectures (Faster R-CNN, YOLOv4) with severe input size
constraints YOLOv4 based studies compress images to 256×768 pixels while Faster R-CNN implementations
typically resize to 600-1099 pixels, yet comparative studies prove that downsampling significantly
impacts performance in analyzing renal pathology. These CNN detectors face inherent architectural
limitations in capturing global context and long range spatial dependencies essential for distinguishing
subtle glomerular pathologies, relying heavily on hand-crafted components like anchor boxes and
non-maximum suppression.
Alternative detection paradigms, particularly transformer-based architectures, remain completely
unexplored for glomerular detection tasks despite their growing adoption in medical imaging. Many
end-to-end systems lack modularity, making it difficult to optimize individual components independently
or identify performance bottlenecks at specific pipeline stages.
Additionally, most works validate on single staining protocols, introducing domain shift
vulnerabilities, while the absence of comprehensive multi-architecture comparative studies across all
three tasks under unified experimental frameworks makes optimal architecture selection difficult.
Critically, limited clinical validation and absence of prospective studies with pathologist-in-the-loop
evaluation restrict the translation of these AI systems into real-world diagnostic workflows, with
most studies reporting only computational metrics without assessing clinical utility, inter-observer
agreement improvement, or diagnostic time reduction. These limitations are systematically analyzed

3.1
Introduction
In this chapter, we present the construction and preprocessing of histopathological datasets
from raw renal biopsy samples collected at CHU Hédi Chaker, Sfax, Tunisia. We detail the complete
pipeline from initial data understanding and quality assessment through privacy protection measures,
annotation workflows, and task-specific preprocessing strategies that transform the original dataset
into three specialized configurations supporting glomerular detection, segmentation, and severity
classification tasks.
Some figures in this chapter show screenshots from the original renal biopsy dataset.
All critical personal identifiers, such as patient names and dates, have been blurred to ensure
confidentiality. The images are presented only for illustrative purposes.
3.2
Data Understanding
3.2.1
Data Source and Composition
The data used in this project comes from a retrospective collection of renal biopsy samples
performed at CHU Hédi Chaker, Sfax, Tunisia, between 2005 and 2014.
The hospital registry
contained a total of 261 patient records, but only 37 patients were included in the provided dataset.
The files were organized into 43 patient folders: 40 folders contained patch-based biopsy images,
and 3 folders contained glomerulus-only images.
After curation, the dataset included 168 patch images linked to 29 patients, in addition
to several follow-up biopsy instances. In total, 11 biopsies came from 5 patients with follow-up
examinations (one patient had three biopsies, while the others had two each). A patient record file
in Doc format accompanied the images, containing fields such as biopsy date, histological diagnosis,
patient ID number, and basic demographic identifiers (name and surname). This file served as the
reference for linking biopsy images to patient-level clinical data.

tissue sections were stained using standard histopathological staining techniques, including Hematoxylin

4.1
Introduction
This chapter details the model architectures and tuning strategies for our three stage
diagnostic pipeline.
We present the selected models for glomeruli detection, segmentation, and
severity classification, justifying each choice based on performance and clinical utility. The subsequent

4.2

For each stage, models were selected based on performance, computational efficiency, and clinical
applicability. The following subsections present the rationale for each choice and summarize the
advantages and limitations.
4.2.1

performance in medical imaging applications. Its mature framework and suitability for resource

• YOLOv11: Incorporates the latest architectural improvements over YOLOv8 while maintaining
efficient inference. Including this version allows us to evaluate whether recent YOLO advances
provide meaningful benefits for glomerular detection tasks.
• RF-DETR: A transformer based detector utilizing self attention mechanisms to capture long
range dependencies. Histopathology images contain complex patterns and subtle variations
requiring robust feature extraction. RF-DETR’s superior spatial reasoning makes it particularly
effective for crowded regions and staining variations, allowing us to assess whether transformer
capabilities justify higher computational costs.

4.3.1.1
YOLOv8
YOLOv8 is a single-stage detector that balances speed and accuracy. As shown in Figure 4.1,
its architecture comprises three components.
The Backbone extracts hierarchical features using
convolutional layers, C2f modules, and a Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF) module for multi-scale
context. The Neck employs a Feature Pyramid Network with Path Aggregation Network (FPN-PAN)
for bidirectional feature fusion. The Head uses three detection branches at different resolutions for
multi-scale prediction. YOLOv8 implements anchor-free detection, simplifying training for diverse
object sizes.

YOLOv11

innovation is the C2PSA module in the neck, which incorporates spatial attention to focus on
relevant regions. The Neck maintains the FPN-PAN structure but uses C3K2 blocks for improved

efficiently.
The Decoder employs learned object queries that gather relevant features through
attention mechanisms.
This end-to-end approach eliminates duplicate predictions via bipartite
matching, making it robust for complex scenarios with overlapping glomeruli.

SAM (Segment Anything Model)
SAM is a foundation model for general purpose segmentation across diverse image types.
As shown in Figure 4.4, its architecture consists of three main components. The Image Encoder uses

4.4.2.2.1
Fine-Tuning Strategy.
Initial baseline experiments using a frozen vision encoder with only Binary Cross Entropy
loss achieved limited validation Dice scores of 0.75–0.80. Three main issues were identified: frozen

encoder preventing feature adaptation, BCE loss focusing on background pixels, and uniform learning

We developed a custom strategy with five modifications:
• Layer Freezing Strategy: We froze the first nine encoder blocks (0–8) to preserve fundamental
visual features that transfer across domains. Early experiments showed that unfreezing more
layers caused catastrophic forgetting, producing noisy and fragmented segmentations. Only
the final three blocks (9–11) were unfrozen to enable adaptation to tissue structures and
glomerular morphology. Fewer unfrozen blocks limited domain adaptation, while more layers
led to overfitting. The mask decoder and prompt encoder remained fully trainable, as they
must respectively learn to generate accurate glomerular boundaries and interpret bounding
boxes in histopathology context.
• Differentiated Learning Rates: Instead of a single learning rate, component-specific rates
were used based on adaptation needs:
Encoder blocks (9–11): 110−5,
Mask decoder: 110−4,
Prompt encoder: 510−5
Higher encoder rates (510−5–110−4) quickly degraded pretrained representations, while lower
decoder rates slowed convergence, requiring 80–100 epochs for acceptable results. The chosen
values emerged from systematic testing across multiple orders of magnitude.
• Combined Loss Function: Standard SAM training uses only BCE, which proved inadequate
for medical segmentation with variable glomerulus sizes and complex boundaries. We designed
a combined three-term objective:
Total Loss = 1.0Dice + 0.5BCE + 0.5Focal
Dice loss measures holistic region overlap, making it robust to class imbalance between small
glomeruli and background. BCE adds pixel-level feedback for sharper boundaries. Focal loss
(α = 0.25, γ = 2.0) emphasizes difficult examples and improves boundary precision. Iterative
experiments showed that Dice alone produced coarse edges, Dice+BCE improved sharpness
but biased small glomeruli, while adding Focal resolved both issues.

4.5.1.1
ResNet50
ResNet50 employs residual learning to facilitate gradient flow in deep networks. Its core
innovation is the residual block, which learns a residual mapping F(x) = H(x)−x instead of a direct
transformation. This is achieved via skip connections that bypass one or more layers, mitigating the
vanishing gradient problem and enabling effective training of its 50-layer architecture.
4.5.1.2
DenseNet121
DenseNet121 introduces dense connectivity, where each layer receives feature maps from all
preceding layers within a dense block via concatenation. This promotes feature reuse, strengthens
gradient propagation, and improves parameter efficiency, achieving strong performance with only 8
million parameters.
4.5.1.3
EfficientNetB0
EfficientNetB0 utilizes a compound scaling method to uniformly balance network depth,
width, and input resolution. Its architecture is built with mobile inverted bottleneck (MBConv)
blocks and incorporates squeeze-and-excitation attention for computational efficiency. With only
5.3 million parameters, it offers an excellent accuracy-efficiency trade-off, making it suitable for
clinical deployment.
4.5.1.4
VGG16
VGG16 is a classical architecture characterized by its simplicity, using only stacks of
33 convolutional and 22 max-pooling layers. However, its 138 million parameters make it highly
susceptible to overfitting on small datasets, serving here as a baseline for understanding the impact
of model size and complexity.
4.5.1.5
InceptionV3
InceptionV3 processes data in parallel at multiple scales using inception modules with

4.5.2
Training Configuration
The training process was organized into two major stages. In the first stage, all fuve models
were compared using standardized training protocols to identify which architecture performed best.
In the second stage, the best performing model received additional architectural modifications and
more sophisticated training strategies to further improve its performance.
4.5.2.1
Stage 1: Multi-Model Comparison
All five architectures were trained using a two-phase transfer learning strategy. Transfer
learning leverages knowledge learned from a large dataset (ImageNet in our case) and adapts it to
a new task (glomerular severity classification). This approach is particularly valuable for medical
imaging where labeled datasets are typically small. The two-phase strategy was designed to prevent
overfitting on our small, imbalanced glomerular dataset while still taking advantage of the pretrained
ImageNet features.
4.5.2.1.1
Phase 1: Feature Extraction (10 epochs)
In the first phase, we froze all the pretrained ImageNet weights in the base model and
trained only the custom classification head that we added on top. Freezing weights means preventing
them from being updated during training. This is important because the pretrained weights already
contain valuable knowledge about general visual features like edges, textures, and object parts. By
freezing these weights, we preserve this knowledge while adapting only the classification head to our
specific task.
This phase used a relatively high learning rate of 10−3 (0.001) with the Adam optimizer.
The Adam optimizer is a popular choice because it adapts the learning rate for each parameter
automatically. We configured it with β1 = 0.9 and β2 = 0.999, which are standard values that
control how the optimizer accumulates gradients over time. The higher learning rate in this phase
allows the randomly initialized classification head to adapt quickly to the glomerular severity task
without disrupting the carefully pretrained feature representations in the frozen base model.

We used several training callbacks to control the training process:
• ModelCheckpoint:
This callback automatically saved the model weights whenever the
validation loss improved.
This ensures we keep the best version of the model rather than
the final version, which might have started overfitting.
• ReduceLROnPlateau: This callback monitors the validation loss and reduces the learning
rate by a factor of 0.5 if the loss stops improving for 5 consecutive epochs. Reducing the
learning rate helps the model make finer adjustments and potentially escape local minima in
the loss landscape.
• EarlyStopping: This callback stops training if the validation loss does not improve for
10 consecutive epochs and restores the best weights. This prevents wasting computational
resources and reduces the risk of overfitting.
4.5.2.1.2
Phase 2: Fine-tuning (40 epochs)
In the second phase, we unfroze the entire model to allow all weights to be updated during
training. However, we kept batch normalization layers frozen. Batch normalization layers maintain
running statistics (mean and variance) that were calculated on ImageNet during pretraining. Keeping
these layers frozen preserves these pretrained statistics and helps prevent a problem called internal
covariate shift, which can destabilize training.
This phase used a much lower learning rate of 10−5 (0.00001) to allow careful, gradual
adaptation of the pretrained features without causing catastrophic forgetting. Catastrophic forgetting
occurs when a pretrained model forgets useful knowledge it learned on the original task while
adapting to the new task. The low learning rate makes small, careful updates that preserve most of
the pretrained knowledge while still adapting to glomerular images.
We also applied a cosine decay schedule with α = 0.1, which gradually reduces the learning
rate over time following a cosine curve. The learning rate at any training step is calculated as:

4.5.2.1.3
Training Configuration Details:
Several important configuration choices were made for Stage 1:
• Data Augmentation: We did not apply any online data augmentation during training.
This decision was made because extensive offline augmentation had already been performed
during the preprocessing stage, including rotation, elastic deformation, color perturbations,
and noise injection.
Applying additional augmentation during training would compound
these transformations, potentially creating unrealistic distortions and degrading image quality.
When transformations are applied multiple times, the cumulative effect can move images too
far from realistic glomerular appearance.
• Regularization: We used dropout with a rate of 0.5 in the classification head, which means
that during each training step, 50% of connections were randomly dropped. This forces the
network to learn robust features that do not depend on any specific subset of neurons. We also
applied L2 weight decay with strength 10−4, which adds a penalty term to the loss function that
discourages large weight values. As mentioned earlier, batch normalization layers remained
frozen throughout the fine-tuning phase.
• Batch Size: We used a batch size of 16, which means 16 images were processed together in
each training step. This value was chosen based on available GPU memory. Larger batch sizes
provide more stable gradient estimates but require more memory.
• Loss Function: We used binary cross-entropy loss, which is the standard choice for binary
classification problems. This loss function measures how well the predicted probabilities match
the true labels.

Enhanced Classification Head Architecture
We replaced the simple two-layer classification head with a more sophisticated architecture:
1. Dual Pooling Strategy: Applied both Global Average Pooling (GAP) and Global Max
Pooling (GMP) in parallel. GAP captures average activations representing general feature
presence, while GMP captures maximum activations representing strongest feature responses.
2. Feature Concatenation:
Concatenated outputs from both pooling operations, yielding
20482 = 4096 features combining average and peak activation information.
3. First Dense Block: Dense layer with 512 units followed by batch normalization, dropout
(0.4), and L2 regularization.
4. Attention Mechanism: Element-wise sigmoid gating enabling adaptive feature weighting
(soft feature selection).
5. Second Dense Block: Dense layer with 256 units followed by batch normalization, dropout
(0.5), and L2 regularization.
6. Final Classification Layer: Sigmoid activation with dropout (0.3) and L2 regularization.
This architecture increases classification capacity while employing multiple regularization
techniques (dropout, L2 regularization, batch normalization) to prevent overfitting.

5.2.1.1

tubules, artifacts, or blood vessels).
• False Negative (FN): A ground truth glomerulus is missed by the detection model, with no
corresponding predicted box achieving sufficient overlap.

Segmentation performance was assessed using standard metrics that quantify pixel-wise

Dice = 2|P ∩G|
|P| + |G|
(5.7)
where P represents predicted pixels and G represents ground truth pixels. Dice scores
range from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect agreement), with values above 0.90 considered excellent for
medical image segmentation.
Intersection over Union (IoU) quantifies the ratio of overlapping area to total area
covered by both masks:
IoU = |P ∩G|
|P ∪G|
(5.8)
IoU is more sensitive to segmentation errors than Dice score, providing a stricter assessment
of boundary precision. Values above 0.85 indicate high-quality segmentation suitable for clinical
applications.
Pixel Accuracy measures the proportion of correctly classified pixels:
Accuracy =
TP + TN
TP + TN + FP + FN
(5.9)
While accuracy provides overall performance indication, it can be misleading in scenarios
with class imbalance, where background pixels vastly outnumber foreground glomerular pixels.

5.4.1.1
Core Performance Metrics
The classification stage was evaluated using standard metrics previously introduced in
Sections 5.2.1.2 and 5.3.1, namely accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. These measures were
complemented by the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), which
provides an additional view of the model’s discriminative ability.
Accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score were computed based on the confusion matrix as
detailed earlier. They assess, respectively, the overall correctness of predictions, the reliability of
positive classifications, the ability to identify all positive cases, and the balance between precision
and recall. In this classification context, the severe class represents positive samples, making recall
particularly critical to ensure that few severe cases are missed.
5.4.1.2
AUC-ROC
The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) quantifies the
model’s capacity to distinguish between severe and not severe cases across all possible decision
thresholds. It is computed as the area under the ROC curve, which plots the True Positive Rate
(TPR) against the False Positive Rate (FPR):
TPR =
TP
TP + FN ,
FPR =
FP
FP + TN .
A higher AUC indicates better discriminative performance, with values close to 1.0 reflecting strong
separation between the two severity levels, while a value of 0.5 corresponds to random guessing.
Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive and balanced evaluation of classification

6.3
System Architecture Overview
The architecture of the Diagnostic Assistive Lupus Nephritis (DALN) system is designed
to support clinical workflows while maintaining rigorous security and data integrity standards.
The system follows a structured, user-centered process that aligns with established pathological
examination procedures.
6.3.1
Workflow Description
The DALN system enforces a sequential workflow beginning with user authentication.
This initial security layer requires valid credentials prior to granting access to any patient data or
diagnostic functions, thereby preventing unauthorized interaction with sensitive medical information.
Following authentication, users proceed to clinical data management, which supports two
distinct pathways. For new patients, clinicians complete a registration process that captures demographic
information and creates an encrypted record in the system database.
For existing patients, the
system enables retrieval of historical biopsies and previous diagnostic records.
Both pathways
converge at the biopsy management stage, where users either upload new tissue samples with
automatic encryption or access previously stored biopsies through secure in memory decryption.
Once a biopsy is selected and parameters are configured, including optional filters for
partial glomeruli exclusion, the system initiates the core artificial intelligence processing pipeline.
This automated phase executes the three-stage analytical cascade detailed in Section 6.4, comprising
glomerular detection via RF-DETR, boundary segmentation using SAM, and severity classification
through ResNet50. Throughout this process, progress indicators provide users with real-time feedback
on system operations. Upon completion, the interface presents comprehensive diagnostic results
including quantitative metrics, severity distribution visualizations, and the final ISN/RPS classification.
6.3.2
Human Validation Integration
A critical component of the DALN architecture is the explicit incorporation of pathologist
oversight.
Following automated analysis, clinicians can inspect individual glomeruli through a
dedicated Image Inspector interface that displays regions of interest with color-coded severity annotations.
If modifications are necessary, such as correcting misclassifications, adjusting detection boundaries,

or removing false positives, the system recalculates all dependent metrics in real time, including
severity ratios and final class determinations. This iterative refinement cycle continues until the
pathologist confirms diagnostic accuracy, at which point the validated diagnosis is committed to the
encrypted database as a permanent, auditable record linked to specific patient and biopsy identifiers.
6.3.3
Architectural Rationale
This workflow design serves three primary objectives. First, it establishes comprehensive
security and traceability measures throughout the diagnostic process, from initial authentication
through encrypted data storage. Second, it clearly separates automated AI processing from human
validation, positioning the system as a diagnostic assistant rather than an autonomous decision-maker.
Third, it maintains flexibility for both prospective analysis of new biopsies and retrospective review
of existing records, supporting diverse clinical use cases within a unified interface.
The architectural representation illustrates the global system workflow rather than detailing
the internal AI pipeline structure, which is documented separately in Section 6.4. While the AI
Processing component abstracts the three-stage analytical cascade for diagrammatic simplicity,
the complete technical implementation, including input standardization, adaptation units, and
result aggregation, is thoroughly documented in subsequent sections. This high-level representation
prioritizes user experience and clinical workflow integration, providing stakeholders with a clear
understanding of how the DALN system functions within laboratory practice.

6.4
The Core Processing Pipeline
This section details the functional flow and integration logic of the DALN diagnostic
pipeline. The architecture follows a strictly modular design, comprising seven sequential Processing
Units. Each unit is responsible for a specific transformation, ensuring traceability, data fidelity, and
clinical interpretability throughout the diagnostic chain.
At the core of the system lies the main control flow, which integrates the three primary AI
components (RF-DETR, SAM, and ResNet50) via the intermediate Adaptation Units (AU1, AU2).
The complete end-to-end processing path forms a linear, reproducible workflow:
Figure 6.8: AI pipeline
The specific function of each of the seven Processing Units is detailed sequentially in the
following subsections.
6.4.1
Input Standardization Unit (ISU)
The Input Standardization Unit (ISU) serves as the pipeline’s secure gateway, ensuring all
incoming biopsy images are unambiguously identified, safely handled, and spatially standardized
prior to processing.
• Data Integrity and Traceability The ISU ensures strict compliance with clinical data
protection by handling encrypted images.
Files are stored exclusively in encrypted form

on disk and are decrypted only in memory using robust, application-level key management,
guaranteeing that no plaintext is ever persisted. For end-to-end traceability, the ISU binds
each image to a three-level, standardized identifier hierarchy (Patient UID →Biopsy UID →
Patch UID). These UIDs are deterministically issued, registered in the database, and used to
standardize filenames, enabling unambiguous cross-referencing in all downstream units.
• Geometric Normalization To guarantee consistent inference results across heterogeneous
inputs of varying original dimensions, the ISU standardizes all images to a target size of
1024×1024. This is achieved through aspect-ratio preserving scaling followed by symmetric
black padding. The parameters necessary for coordinate inversion (scaling factor and padding)
are recorded as metadata, ensuring that all subsequent model outputs (detections, masks, and
classifications) can be accurately reversed to align with the original biopsy coordinates for
clinical interpretation.
• Outcome The ISU outputs a securely decrypted image tensor in the standardized 1024×1024
geometry, along with a complete metadata record containing all linkage keys and inversion
parameters.
6.4.2
Detection Stage (RF-DETR)
The Detection Unit’s sole function is to execute the RF-DETR model inference on the
standardized image tensor. This step identifies potential glomeruli by predicting raw bounding box
coordinates (x1, y1, x2, y2) and confidence scores within the 1024×1024 space. The unit outputs
these raw predictions to the subsequent adaptation stage for quality control and formatting.
6.4.3
Adaptation Unit 1 (AU1)
Adaptation Unit 1 acts as the critical quality control and data bridge between the detection
and segmentation stages. It performs two primary operations:
• Partial Glomeruli Filtering The patch-based image acquisition protocol often results in
glomeruli being truncated at image boundaries, leading to incomplete pathological context.
This crucial filtering mechanism is applied to the raw detection outputs to address this
acquisition artifact. The filter’s primary role is to reject severely truncated or boundary-cut

glomeruli while retaining those that, despite being partially cut, maintain a sufficient coverage
ratio and exceed a high statistical confidence threshold. This ensures that the resource-intensive
SAM segmentor operates only on clinically reliable regions of interest.
• Data Transformation and Transfer Following filtering, the unit converts the refined list of
bounding box coordinates and scores into the structured PyTorch tensor format (specifically,
the required [1,N,4] shape).
All transformed data is then efficiently moved to the CUDA
device to ensure that the Segmentation Unit receives data in the optimal format and location
for batch processing, thereby minimizing transfer overhead.
6.4.4
Segmentation Stage (SAM)
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) receives bounding boxes as prompts and produces
probability masks delineating glomerular boundaries. Its transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture
ensures contextual precision. The generated logits (256 × 256) are upsampled via bilinear interpolation
to 1024×1024, maintaining boundary smoothness and sub-pixel accuracy.
6.4.5
Adaptation Unit 2 (AU2)
AU2 restores the spatial alignment between AI predictions and the original biopsy image.
It performs four ordered steps:
1. Inverse Geometric Mapping:
Original coordinates are recovered from the standardized 1024×1024 space using ISU metadata:

2. Mask Restoration:
The predicted mask is binarized (threshold = 0.5) and resized to (W0×H0) using nearest-neighbor
interpolation to preserve binary fidelity.
3. Precise ROI Cropping:
The restored mask is applied to the original image to isolate the glomerulus.
Only pixels
inside the mask are retained, while the background is set to black (0, 0, 0), producing a clean,
anatomically localized crop.

6.5
Parallel and Pipelined Processing Optimization
To maximize hardware utilization and reduce overall latency, the DALN pipeline adopts
a pipelined parallel execution model that overlaps the three AI stages, detection, segmentation,
and classification across multiple images. Instead of processing one image sequentially through all
stages, the system processes several images concurrently, so that while one image is being detected,
another is segmented, and a third is classified. This concurrency is managed by three dedicated
thread pools, each configured with two workers, and interconnected through asynchronous FIFO
queues that decouple stage execution. The Detection Queue feeds bounding boxes and confidence
scores to the segmentation stage, the Segmentation Queue passes generated masks and regions of
interest to the classifier, and the Results Queue collects per-image outputs for aggregation. This
asynchronous design ensures that throughput depends only on the slowest stage rather than the
cumulative latency of all three.
Fault tolerance is handled at the per-image level, allowing any
failed image to be isolated without interrupting the rest of the batch. Table 6.3 summarizes the
worker configuration and queue communication, and Figure 6.9 illustrates the temporal overlap
of the pipeline for two images, showing that detection starts concurrently, segmentation tasks are
longer, and classification follows immediately per image.

6.6
Data Management and Security Framework
Given that the system is intended for deployment within medical laboratories, where patient
data must remain confidential and operations often occur in secure offline environments, robust local
data protection is essential.
To meet these requirements, the current version of the system employs SQLCipher, a
high security relational database engine providing full AES-256 encryption of all stored data.
This ensures that sensitive medical information such as biopsy metadata, diagnostic outcomes, and
patients informations remains protected at rest and inaccessible without authorization keys.
SQLCipher offers strong encryption transparency, enabling secure transactional operations,
indexing, and data retrieval without exposing plaintext information at any point.
This design
supports a fully secure, autonomous workflow suitable for laboratory desktop applications, where
data integrity, confidentiality, and reliability are fundamental.
6.6.1
Database Architecture and Schema Design
The data architecture is implemented on SQLCipher, utilizing a relational structure designed
to map the clinical data flow onto the AI processing pipeline.
This structure enforces a strict,
five-tiered hierarchy from the patient record down to the granular AI prediction, ensuring that every
result is traceable back to its originating image and patient identifier.
For a complete visualization of these relationships, including entity cardinality and key
constraints, refer to the Entity-Relationship Diagram provided in Appendix B 6.8 Figure 7.2.
The core schema is built upon five primary entities, with data integrity maintained via
105

foreign keys (FOREIGN KEY) and uniqueness enforced via UID fields (UNIQUE):
• Patients: This root table stores non-clinical demographic data and establishes the primary
linkage key. Key fields include a globally unique identifier (patient_uid) and personal information
(full_name,age).
• Biopsies: Represents a single clinical session or sample.
It links directly to the Patients
table via patient_id and stores a persistent system identifier (biopsy_uid) used for internal
traceability.
• Patches: This table links the database structure to the physical, encrypted image files on disk.
It is vital for traceability, recording image-specific metadata such as the file hash (file_hash),
the encrypted storage path (encrypted_path), and a unique patch identifier (patch_uid).
• Glomeruli: The most granular level, holding the raw AI prediction outputs.
Each row
represents a single detected and classified glomerulus, linked to a specific patch (patch_id).
Critical fields include the predicted severity class (predicted_class), the confidence score, and
the geometric coordinates (coords).
• Diagnoses: Serves as the final aggregation and audit record for the AI analysis. It holds the
summary statistics for the entire biopsy (biopsy_id is UNIQUE), including total_glomeruli,
severity_rate, avg_confidence, and the final_class output.
This structure ensures that a
biopsy can only have one current, validated AI summary, which is overwritten during re-analysis
via controlled UPSERT transactions.
6.6.2
Database Optimization and Consistency Control
Database efficiency is maintained through targeted indexing on frequently queried fields
(e.g., patient–biopsy linkage, biopsy–glomerulus mapping, and file hashes). This ensures sub-second
query response times even for large datasets.
All write operations occur within transactional contexts, ensuring atomicity and preventing
partial updates. Diagnostic re-analyses use controlled UPSERT operations, replacing outdated entries
to maintain a single validated result per biopsy.
Automated timestamps support traceability and auditability, while unique and non-null
constraints enforce data validity across all entities.

Regular encrypted backups and optional point-in-time recovery using SQLCipher

6.6.3
Security and Data Protection
All stored information is encrypted using AES-256 via SQLCipher. The encryption key
is securely managed as an environment variable, ensuring that the database remains inaccessible
without authorization.
Medical images are encrypted individually with Fernet (AES-128 CBC) and stored
under hashed filenames to preserve privacy. Decryption occurs only in memory during analysis,
ensuring that unencrypted files are never written to disk. Temporary data generated during inference
is securely deleted upon task completion, minimizing the risk of residual data exposure.

• Strict access control and authentication.
• Audit logs for all data access and modification events.

such as HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, USA) for medical

General Conclusion
This project has successfully demonstrated the design, development, and validation of an
end-to-end AI assisted diagnostic system for Lupus Nephritis. Beginning with a clear clinical need
identified at the CHU Hedi Chaker in Sfax, we established a robust methodological framework
that integrated the CRISP-DM process with a Data-Centric AI approach. This foundation guided
the systematic progression from data understanding and preparation through to the modeling and
evaluation of sophisticated deep learning architectures.
The core of our work resulted in a high-performance, multi-stage pipeline. For glomeruli
detection, the RF-DETR model proved superior, leveraging its transformer based architecture to
achieve exceptional accuracy and recall.
The Segment Anything Model (SAM) was selected for
the segmentation stage, delivering precise pixel level boundaries crucial for morphological analysis.
Finally, an enhanced ResNet50 architecture was developed for severity classification, achieving a
high level of accuracy in distinguishing between severe and non-severe glomeruli, thereby providing
direct support for ISN/RPS Class III and IV classification. The integration of these components
into a unified, secure, and optimized system marks a significant step towards a practical tool for
computational pathology.
This project represents an important milestone within the broader context of the Tunisian
Nephropathology Digitalization Initiative. While the current system serves as a fully functional
diagnosis interface for laboratory use, our future work will focus on expanding both its diagnostic
capabilities and clinical integration. A key priority is extending the system’s classification scope to
include Class II (Mesangial) and Class V (Membranous) Lupus Nephritis, with our medical partners
already preparing the necessary immunofluorescence biopsy samples for this expansion.
We are committed to completing the comprehensive user interface, including a fully functional
sign-up interface with all necessary controls for user roles, positions, and account approval workflows,
as well as full user management capabilities and a complete patient management system to track
medical histories and diagnoses.
These developments are essential for integrating the tool into
routine clinical workflows. Looking further ahead, we plan to develop an online platform that will
allow authorized physicians across different healthcare facilities to securely upload biopsy images and
receive AI-assisted analysis, supporting telepathology initiatives and expanding access to specialized

Abstract
The DALN (Diagnostic Assistant for Lupus Nephritis) system addresses the slow manual
diagnostic process faced by pathologists analyzing renal biopsy images. Developed as a final-year
project at the Higher Institute of Computer Science in Mahdia, in collaboration with the renal
pathology lab at CHU Hedi Chaker, Sfax, the system leverages artificial intelligence to automate
biopsy image analysis.
Using Python and deep learning libraries such as TensorFlow and
PyTorch, it accurately detects glomeruli, outlines their boundaries, and classifies lesion severity.
This work contributes to a national initiative to digitize pathology services in Tunisia, providing
pathologists with a tool to enhance diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.
Keywords :
Lupus Nephritis, artificial intelligence, diagnostic assistant, pathologist, digitization

Le système Assistant Diagnostique pour la Néphrite Lupique DALN répond au problème de
